# Summary: At the Limit of Distinction

## Core Claim

Scientific entities such as electrons, spin, charge and mass are *semantic agents*: named model components constructed from registered measurement regularities and subsequently granted causal, explanatory or behavioural agency within scientific language. The monograph reconstructs the full trajectory by which physical interactions at the generonic boundary become observable quantum quantities, and identifies two foundational limits — the Greene–Pascal Limit and the Alphonic Limit — that mark where symbolic decomposition diverges from independently measured physical distinction.

---

## Structure

The monograph is organised in eight parts and twenty-six chapters, with a Notes on Terminology section and Selected References.

### Part I: The Measured World Begins with a Distinction (Chs. 1–3)

The measured world originates at the *generonic boundary*: the point at which an unresolved physical interaction produces a physically instantiated, retainable distinction capable of entering a symbolic system. The elementary empirical event is a transition within a registering system — not possession of the unresolved world itself. A key structural inequality separates measurement form from ontological conclusion:

> stepwise measurability ≠⇒ stepwise ontology. (1.2)

The formal chain from interaction to observable is written:

> W̃ —G→ ΔR —E→ sᵢ —M→ Õᵢ (1.3)

where W̃ is the unresolved interactional world, G the generonic boundary, ΔR the physical alteration of the measuring system, E the encoding operation, sᵢ the finite symbol, and M the model through which the symbol becomes an observable Õᵢ.

A single registration establishes one distinction (D̃₁: 0→1). Measured plurality requires at least two non-identical registrations remaining admissible within the complete measurement arrangement (D̃₂: r̃₁ ≠ r̃₂), with the separability condition d(r̃₁, r̃₂) > δ_system. Every physically instantiated symbol has extent: a finite state is S̃ᵢ = (Gᵢ, tᵢ, δᵢ, Hᵢ, Cᵢ), preserving geometry, time, uncertainty, provenance and consensus conditions.

### Part II: From Registration to Semantic Agent (Chs. 4–7)

Measurement is selective compression: I → ΔR → sᵢ, with sᵢ ≠ Ĩ (4.2). The observable is a finite symbolic achievement — produced through a chain joining physical interaction, instrument response, encoding, calibration and model — rather than a pre-existing feature found directly. Its construction trajectory is:

> interaction → apparatus change → signal → datum → observable. (5.1)

A *semantic agent* is a named component constructed from registered regularities to which causal, explanatory or behavioural agency is subsequently assigned. The general trajectory of agent construction is:

> {registrations} → stable relation → parameter → name → property bearer → causal agent. (6.1)

The *Greene–Pascal semantic split* describes the act of dividing a relational registration into separately named explanatory components. Chapter 7 develops the Greene–Pascal conjunction: Robert Greene's indefinite symbolic divisibility of the world and Pascal's inability of language to ground every division through additional language together show how symbolic resolution may continue after physical resolution has failed.

### Part III: Charge, Mass and the Point-Agent (Chs. 8–9)

The charge–mass distinction is not presented by experiment as two independently observed substances. A critical FSM inequality applies:

> symbolic separability ≠⇒ measured physical separability. (8.1)

The electron is assembled as a persistent bearer to which charge, mass and later spin are attached — each performing explanatory work — but the completed noun conceals the sequence of experiments and consensus decisions through which properties were separated. Chapter 9 examines the point-agent: the transition from "no resolved internal structure" (indexed to instrument, energy range and resolution criterion) to "extensionless point-agent" (unindexed model commitment) is a compression, not a measurement result. FSM retains the formal point as a permitted instrument within a symbolic calculus while refusing to identify it with a physically instantiated primitive.

### Part IV: Stern–Gerlach as a Provenance Study (Chs. 10–13)

The Stern–Gerlach experiment receives extended treatment as a case where the passage from physical registration to semantic agent can be reconstructed with unusual clarity. The primary experimental trajectory is:

> source + prepared silver beam + collimation + field geometry + flight interval + detector → finite deposition distribution. (10.1)

The apparatus registers a finite spatial distribution (not an intrinsic property). Classification converts that distribution into a binary alphabet:

> finite spatial distributions —C→ {+, −}. (11.1)

The inferential movement from detector distribution to spin-½ contains several commitments:

> two outcome regions → two-valued magnetic response → angular-momentum component → electron spin → spin-½ particle. (12.1)

The familiar s = ½ and Sz = ±ℏ/2 enter through mathematical structure involving the angular-momentum algebra, the chosen measurement direction and Planck's reduced constant — not as directly registered marks. Chapter 13 traces the *transfer of agency*:

> apparatus-system interaction → particle → spin → outcome. (13.1)

Agency originally distributed across the whole arrangement is progressively concentrated into a selected object and then into a property of that object.

### Part V: Metrology and the Limit of Distinction (Chs. 14–16)

Three metrological concepts must be kept distinct if the Greene–Pascal boundary is to be located:

- **Spatial resolution** (δ_instrument): minimum separation at which two features can be distinguished.
- **Positional precision** (δ_parameter): repeatability or estimated uncertainty with which the location of a fitted feature can be assigned. A fitted parameter can satisfy δ_parameter < δ_instrument without implying the independently resolvable distinction has the same scale.
- **Model-relative accuracy**: agreement with an independently warranted reference.

X-ray crystallography illustrates reconstruction: the atomic structure is obtained through crystal interaction → diffraction intensities → encoded pattern → reconstructed density → atomic coordinates (15.1). The phrase "astonishing accuracy" is analysed as a rhetorical semantic agent that discourages renewed inspection of the measurement chain. The correct FSM statement is:

> |x̃_calculated − x̃_measured| ≤ δ̃, (16.2)

where each term retains its provenance.

### Part VI: The Greene–Pascal and Alphonic Limits (Chs. 17–19)

The **Greene–Pascal Limit** is defined provisionally as a function of the complete measurement and interpretive system:

> G_P = G_P(I, M, E, C, H), (17.1)

where I is the instrument, M the model, E the encoding process, C the consensus conditions and H the measurement history. It marks the boundary beyond which continued symbolic decomposition no longer corresponds to independently sustained physical distinctions.

The **Alphonic Limit** (α) concerns a more foundational boundary: the minimum finite physical extent at which a distinction can be instantiated, preserved and admitted into a symbolic system. Its relation to the Greene–Pascal Limit is expressed by:

> α ≤ G_P(I, M, E, C, H). (18.1)

Chapter 19 examines whether the two limits converge. Three possibilities are held open: G_P > α (distinct limits), G_P → α (asymptotic approach), or G_P ≡ α (same boundary viewed from opposite directions). The decision requires careful empirical and conceptual work and must not be settled by linguistic compression.

### Part VII: Finite Symbolic Dynamics as a Reconstruction (Chs. 20–22)

FSD offers an alternative to particle-centred construction. Its central empirical object is a trajectory of provenance-bearing registrations:

> Γ̃ = (S̃₀, S̃₁, …, S̃ₙ). (20.1)

The constructive order is: finite trajectories → stable relational invariants → optional semantic agent (20.2). Semantic agents may be retained when useful, provided they remain attached to their trajectory class. A provenance record for a semantic agent takes the form:

> Ã = (N, R, E, M, K, U), (21.1)

where N is the name, R the class of supporting registrations, E the encoding trajectory, M the mathematical model, K the added commitments and U the unresolved alternatives and uncertainties.

The *agency-and-provenance audit* (Ch. 22) is proposed as a systematic research method: for each quantum concept, trace from the physical alteration of the apparatus through every transformation to the final explanatory sentence, and identify what disappeared along the way.

### Part VIII: Consequences and Future Direction (Chs. 23–26)

The complete constructive trajectory is:

> interaction → physical change → registered distinction → encoded symbol → model-defined observable → inferred property → semantic agent → agent explains interaction. (26.1)

The quantum world appears near the end of this sequence, not its beginning. The central criticism is not that quantum physics invents a fictional world but that its semantic agents are frequently detached from their measurement provenance and permitted to explain the registrations from which they were originally inferred.

Prediction without ontological promotion (Ch. 23) insists that formal correspondence P̃ ↔ R̃ does not establish: formal semantic agent = independently observed physical constituent (23.2). The measured world is a *consensus-stabilised world* (Ch. 24): W̃_science = F(W̃, I, E, M, C, H) (24.1). Chapter 25 proposes that the name "quantum mechanics" may eventually require replacement, since it carries inherited commitments to quanta as foundational entities.

## New or Extended FSM Concepts

- **Semantic agent**: named model component constructed from registered regularities, subsequently granted causal/explanatory/behavioural agency. Does not decide whether the agent has a physical referent; preserves how that referent is warranted.
- **Greene–Pascal semantic split**: dividing a relational registration into separately named explanatory components whose history of construction must remain visible.
- **Greene–Pascal Limit**: instrument-, model-, encoding-, consensus- and history-dependent boundary beyond which symbolic decomposition loses correspondence to independently sustained physical distinctions.
- **Alphonic Limit** (α): proposed minimum finite physical extent at which a distinction can be instantiated, preserved and admitted into a symbolic system; lower bound on G_P.
- **First-order non-single-distinction event**: minimum measurement situation in which at least two registrations remain separately admissible (d(r̃₁, r̃₂) > δ_system).
- **Agency-and-provenance audit**: systematic method for recovering the measurement history of a quantum concept by tracing from apparatus alteration to final explanatory sentence.
- **Provenance distance**: number and character of transformations between registration and the final semantic agent (contrasted with operational distance).
