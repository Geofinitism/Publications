# On Removing Uncertainty Using Constants

**Subtitle:** How Setting Numerical Values as Exact Changes the Uncertainty Reported in Scientific Results  
**Running title:** The Anchored Measure  
**Author:** Kevin R. Haylett, Manchester, UK  
**Year:** 2026 | **Pages:** 27 | **Series:** A Monograph in Finite Symbolic Mechanics

---

## Core Claim

Modern metrology fixes the numerical values of selected physical constants — the speed of light in vacuum, the Planck constant, the elementary charge, the caesium hyperfine transition frequency — assigning them exact definitional status within the International System of Units. This monograph argues that this act increases the *symbolic determinacy* of the measurement frame but does not increase the *physical precision* of the encounters through which measurements are made. The central proposition is:

> **increased symbolic determinacy ≠ increased physical precision** (Eq. 4.1)

Fixing a constant removes its uncertainty from formal propagation by converting it from a measured input into a defining condition. The uncertainty does not disappear; it is *relocated* into realisation procedures, environmental corrections, calibration chains, and model assumptions. The published uncertainty of a scientific result is therefore a *residual uncertainty* — what remains after the symbolic frame has been fixed — not the total uncertainty of the physical encounter.

## Structure

The monograph develops its argument across 9 chapters and 3 appendices.

**Chapter 1 — The Setting of the Measure** traces the historical movement from material artefact (the platinum–iridium metre bar) through atomic spectral definitions to the 1983 fixing of the speed of light at c ≡ 299 792 458 m s⁻¹. It distinguishes three concepts: the *exactness of the inscription* (the symbol sequence is fixed), the *definition* (that sequence sets a unit relation), and the *authority of the anchor* (institutional coordination around the fixed value). The metre bar has left the centre of the definition, but physical interaction has not left measurement.

**Chapter 2 — Exact Definition and Finite Realisation** introduces the four locations at which exactness and uncertainty must be distinguished: (1) Inscription — the adopted symbol sequence is fixed; (2) Definition — the SI rules assign it a unit-establishing role; (3) Realisation — every practical enactment is finite, interactional, and uncertain; (4) Model — the physical and mathematical account connecting registrations to reported quantities remains conditional. The chapter distinguishes the *observational statement* x = x̂ ± u_x from the *defining statement* {x}_U ≡ N, and introduces the *closed relation*: once c defines the metre, any discrepancy in measuring c must be assigned to realisation or model, not to a revised SI value of c.

**Chapter 3 — Uncertainty in a Cross-Related System** analyses how uncertainty propagates through a measurement model y = f(x₁, ..., x_n, c, h, e). The first-order combined variance is u²_y ≈ ΣΣ (∂f/∂q_i)(∂f/∂q_j) cov(q_i, q_j). If a constant's uncertainty is set to zero by definition, those variance and covariance terms vanish from the formal propagation. The chapter works through the concrete example of E = mc², showing that (u_E/E)² = (u_m/m)² + 4(u_c/c)² under the empirical architecture reduces to (u_E/E)² ≈ (u_m/m)² under the SI. It warns against naive addition of historical uncertainties (covariance structure must be reconstructed) while insisting that any independent uncertainty admitted as empirical must propagate. The chapter introduces *residual uncertainty*: u_reported = u(Y | F, M, H), where F is the fixed symbolic frame, M is the active measurement model, and H is the accepted history and provenance.

**Chapter 4 — The Effect of the Anchor** examines what the fixed constant does and does not do. A photodetector receives no additional photons because a committee has fixed a number; a clock experiences no reduction in environmental perturbation; an interferometer acquires no improved alignment. The anchor provides a stable symbolic location — it does not refine the physical encounter. When the uncertainty of a defining constant is set to zero, the difficulties of realisation are *relocated* rather than eliminated: frequency realisation, optical path, detector timing, calibration, alignment, material response, and numerical reconstruction remain finite. The chapter develops the *rounded-light thought experiment* — setting c at exactly 300 000 000 m s⁻¹ would produce a new metre 0.0691807% shorter than the existing one, showing that the exact numerical value is conventional and that continuity with historical measurements performs an *epistemic compression* that makes the act of setting less visible.

**Chapter 5 — The Compressed Result** examines the terminal expression X = X̂ ± u_X. This compact form is the end of a much longer trajectory depending upon unit definitions, realisations, calibration standards, sampling procedures, detector responses, corrections, models, statistical assumptions, software, and decisions about which sources of uncertainty are admitted. The chapter introduces *symbolic drag*: a compressed expression appears to carry more certainty than has been inscribed within it, because institutional memory and reader expectation supply the omitted trajectories. The full object of interest is not the pair (X̂, u_X) but a *provenance-bearing result structure*:

> **R = (X̂, u_X, F, M, H, A)** (Eq. 5.2)

where F is the defining frame, M is the measurement model, H is the historical and calibration provenance, and A is the set of admissibility conditions. The short form Y = Ŷ ± u_reported is a projection of R, not its complete form.

**Chapter 6 — Measurement as a Finite Symbolic Trajectory** situates the argument within FSM. Physical interactions occur at sensors, surfaces, junctions, cavities, and crystals; they are converted by analogue-to-digital processes into finite sequences of symbols at the *generonic boundary*. Every subsequent operation — baseline subtraction, calibration, event reconstruction, statistical fitting — acts upon the registered symbolic trajectory. Layers are neither neutral nor arbitrary: the instrument is not a transparent window, nor are its outputs self-confirming. The chapter introduces *conditional significance*: a reported five-sigma result is a statement about the behaviour of a test statistic under a specified null model, not a distance between a sentence and an unmediated world.

**Chapter 7 — An Illustrative Case: The Higgs Result** applies the framework to the 2012 Higgs boson discovery. What was registered was a repeatable family of event structures compatible with a Higgs-like process — not a directly observed particle. The Standard Model functions as a *system attractor*, shaping detector design, event classification, background estimation, and the language of deviations. The chapter distinguishes the technical achievement (the registered interactions can resist the expected trajectory) from the epistemic limitation (the degree to which convergence is imposed by the symbolic and institutional basin remains open). The most careful statement is that multiple finite registration trajectories converged upon a model-compatible semantic entity with residual uncertainties evaluated inside a fixed metrological and statistical system.

**Chapter 8 — Final Discussion: Objections, Replies, and the Remaining Difference** addresses eight objections: (8.1) the constants are definitions, not uncertain inputs; (8.2) restoring uncertainty would double-count existing terms; (8.3) changing a unit cannot alter statistical evidence; (8.4) the necessary conditions are already documented; (8.5) all observation is model-conditioned; (8.6) exact constants are a practical achievement; (8.7) the criticism weakens public confidence; (8.8) the remaining proposition. Each objection is accepted on its own terms and then shown to leave the central proposition intact. The metrological account and the FSM account can coexist, provided definitional exactness is not mistaken for physical exactness.

**Chapter 9 — Conclusion** holds the central conclusion in two connected sentences: a fixed constant does not increase the precision of the physical encounter; it increases the determinacy of the symbolic frame in which that encounter is reported. A published uncertainty is not the uncertainty of an encounter in isolation; it is the residual uncertainty admitted after the measurement system has fixed its defining relations and compressed its supporting trajectory.

**Appendix A — A General Propagation Form** derives the matrix form u²_y ≈ **J**Σ**J**ᵀ, where **J** is the Jacobian row vector and Σ is the covariance matrix of admitted uncertain inputs. A defining constant does not appear as an uncertain component of **q**, or equivalently occupies a component with zero variance and covariance. Restoring it as an empirical quantity expands both **q** and Σ.

## New and Extended FSM Concepts

**Anchored measure** — A measurement expressed within a symbolic frame in which one or more formerly measured constants have been assigned exact definitional values. The result is valid within that architecture but conditional upon its fixed relations.

**Residual uncertainty** — u_reported = u(Y | F, M, H). The uncertainty that remains in a published result after the symbolic frame F, measurement model M, and accepted history H have been fixed. Distinguished from the total uncertainty that would propagate under an empirical architecture.

**Provenance-bearing result structure** — R = (X̂, u_X, F, M, H, A). The complete object of a scientific measurement, including the defining frame, measurement model, historical and calibration provenance, and admissibility conditions. The compact terminal expression X = X̂ ± u_X is a projection of R.

**Symbolic drag** — The apparent capacity of a compressed expression to carry more certainty than has been inscribed within it, arising because institutional memory and reader expectation supply the omitted trajectories. Deepened by the use of exact defining constants, which remove the history of the anchor from the active uncertainty budget.

**Closed relation** — After a constant is fixed as a unit-defining relation, any experimental discrepancy from that value must be assigned to realisation, model, or instrument rather than to a revised SI value. The system is not falsified but changes the form in which the question can be asked.

**Four locations of exactness and uncertainty** — Inscription (fixed symbol sequence), Definition (metrological rule), Realisation (finite, interactional, calibrated, uncertain), Model (conditional on physical, mathematical, computational, and statistical assumptions). The exactness of the first two locations does not migrate automatically into the latter two.

**Relocation rather than elimination** — When a defining constant's uncertainty is set to zero, the associated physical difficulties are redistributed into other terms (frequency realisation, environmental corrections, detector timing, model adequacy), not removed from the physical trajectory.

**Conditional significance** — A statistical significance value such as five sigma is a statement about a test statistic under a specified null model inside a fixed analysis structure, not a measure of distance between a claim and an unmediated world.

**System attractor** — An institutional and symbolic basin that shapes which registrations are made, how deviations are classified, and how results are expressed. Identified as a real feature of high-energy physics but not sufficient to dismiss the results produced within it; the question is the geometry of resistance.
