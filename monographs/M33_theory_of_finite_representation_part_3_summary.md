# A Theory of Finite Representation, Part III: The Geofinite Audit

**Subtitle:** Axioms, Mathematical Objects and Functional Symbolic Trajectories

## Abstract

A formal object may occupy the beginning of an argument while arriving late in the trajectory that made the argument possible. Axioms require a language, distinguishable inscriptions, conventions, readers and histories of use. Mathematical objects gather constructive permissions into names that can travel through further proofs. Bridge theorems make results portable between representations and fields. The resulting mobility is functional, but a compact endpoint does not necessarily carry the distinctions required by every later continuation.

This monograph develops a Geofinite audit of those relationships. It connects the finite representation of Part I with the reports, ledgers and conditional insufficiency of Part II. ZFC is examined through a declared first-order presentation, with each axiom or schema followed through its formal permission, a finite use, its representational commitments and the questions arising in an FSM translation. Selected mathematical objects, algebra, proof dependencies and matrix unfoldings provide further specimens. Alphonic distinction is related to the provisional Greene–Pascal framing of the trajectories needed to sustain its functional use.

The general audit names a construction and its intended continuation, unfolds relevant trajectories, records licences and compressions, examines correspondences, and reports local sufficiency, witnessed insufficiency, representational debt and cost under stated conditions. Worked cases concern infinity, a finite set with two construction histories, a polynomial representation bridge, and the partition of a registered extent. The audit is itself a finite report whose language and stopping conditions remain inspectable. Function is retained throughout: mathematics, measurement, poetry and fiction can each sustain Functional Symbolic Trajectories under different commitments and regions of use.

## Prefatory Context

Part III follows *A Theory of Finite Representation, Part I: Extent, Registration and the Licence to Represent* (M29) and *Part II: Finite Reports, Accounts and Operational Insufficiency* (M32). Its purpose is to turn their representational account into an instrument of inquiry. The principal specimens are classical mathematical constructions and the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms with Choice. The further outcome is a general method for auditing a symbolic construction in relation to the work it is being asked to perform. The Greene–Pascal contribution is introduced as a framing of the Functional Symbolic Trajectory, or supporting region of trajectories, needed to hold and use an Alphonic distinction. The writing follows the revised outline agreed in October 2026, with prologue and conclusion outside the chapter numbering.

## Chapter Summaries

### Prologue: The Foundation at the Top

Opens with the image of a mountain assembled through measurement. As distinctions accumulate, a refined endpoint is called fundamental — it becomes the opening noun of further explanation, and the trajectory of its production and its explanatory use run in different directions. The audit asks what happens when the second trajectory is presented without the first. The inquiry begins with function: mathematics, poetry and instruments each build useful paths through their axioms and objects. The task is to make the enabling commitments visible when the path is asked to continue.

### Chapter 1 — From Representation to Report to Audit

Recalls Part I's instantiated representation (the state *R* = (*s*, α, δ, *H*, *C*)) and Part II's three-path analysis (bead, ledger, classical). The present volume applies this machinery to inherited symbolic constructions. An axiom is inspected through its formal permission and the representational episode carrying its use; a mathematical object through its definition, operations and compressed dependencies; a bridge through the relationships it preserves and the continuations that need those relationships. Representation, report and audit are successive functions within one continuing trajectory, not three completed containers.

### Chapter 2 — Two Starting Positions

Distinguishes the classical pointer (ZFC as the declared formal theory) from FSM's starting position. FSM places the conditions of representation — distinction, extent, uncertainty, provenance and admissibility — at the beginning of its own account. The order of inquiry therefore changes: before asking which operations a named object permits, FSM asks what trajectory holds the name and makes those operations traversable. The burden of carrying is asymmetric: FSM makes the classical construction a rich specimen because its operations can be followed without requiring the report to adopt all its foundational commitments.

### Chapter 3 — Before the Axiom

An axiom begins a derivation under a declared formal licence, but the act of writing, reading or admitting it already has a trajectory that the formal ordering need not display. This trajectory includes grammar, encoding, a reader's trained capacities and institutional history. The audit can reopen it at several depths: formal grammar and source, the permission's historical development, a reader's reconstruction of meaning, or the reliability of an implementation. These are distinct tasks. The diagram Γ_enabling → A → Γ_derivation (Eq. 3.1) records the two functions of the present bead.

### Chapter 4 — The Licence to Infinity

The finite mark ∞ can be registered, copied and incorporated into a proof. The mathematical commitments associated with a particular occurrence must be recovered from its context. An unbounded continuation instruction, an infinite-set assertion and a limit expression need different accounts. A finite successor trace and the inductive-set axiom are not identical: the trace makes specific steps available; the axiom supplies a formal permission concerning an entire set. The FSM profile asks which trace, continuation rule and supporting report are present. Chapter 18 develops the worked audit.

### Chapter 5 — The Container and the Trajectory

A set presents its members under a membership rule. FSM locates the carrying structure in the FST: the set becomes an admitted compression that makes membership relationships available at a stage of that process. Two ledgers producing the same finite collection of labels have different histories; their membership reports agree but their continuation capacities may differ. The Grand Corpus points to the wider accumulated field of symbolic trajectories. The wrapper remains dynamic: new registrations, definitions or readings can alter available continuations.

### Chapter 6 — An FSM Audit of the ZFC Axioms

The full eleven axioms and schemas of ZFC are audited in sequence: Extensionality, Empty Set, Pairing, Union, Power Set, Infinity, Separation, Replacement, Foundation, Choice — plus the gathered profiles (§6.12). Each specimen has a formal statement, an operational reading and an audit focus. The audit records the formal domain, finite episode, compressed distinctions and proposed continuation. It can state that an internal formal move is admitted while an FSM translation remains to be constructed; or that a particular finite implementation already supports a bounded use.

### Chapter 7 — The Classical Mathematical Object

A name gathers relationships into a portable unit. The audit separates the name's inscription from the definition it invokes and the present operation using it. Zero, natural numbers, real numbers, complex quantities, functions, matrices, integrals and groups are surveyed as specimens. Each can be inspected at different resolutions: a theorem may need only domain and an established property; an implementation may need encoding, access order and arithmetic regime; an FSM translation may need a construction that carries distinction, extent, uncertainty and history. The present volume establishes what such a reconstruction would need to disclose, leaving fuller object reconstruction to the separate Mathematical Objects programme.

### Chapter 8 — Algebra as Symbolic Mobility

Algebra makes constructions mobile by supporting substitutions and cancellations under a grammar and a licence. The familiar relation (*a* + *b*) − *b* = *a* (Eq. 8.1) performs an exact job in an appropriate additive structure; its report need not name a particular gold transfer or detector response. In an account of a transfer the retained amount, displaced amount and accessible disposition record can all be relevant. An FSM account follows the conditions under which algebraic mobility can be used and the points at which a later question needs the supporting account to become visible. Algebra's function is recorded positively: it enables substitution, reuse and construction across many paths.

### Chapter 9 — Perfect Division and Finite Distinction

The Alphonic condition identifies where a relevant difference becomes non-single in the present operation. Two candidate states can be admitted as the same code by one operation and separated by another — the latter is a new distinction event that should enter the ledger with its conditions. Uncertainty belongs to a registration or claim under finite conditions; formal identity belongs to a declared symbolic domain. A device specimen (binary threshold logic) shows how finite distinction supports powerful exact symbolic conventions in a local regime, without declaring the code to contain the entire event.

### Chapter 10 — Greene–Pascal: Representational Support

Introduces the Greene–Pascal contribution as a provisional framing: the FST, or supporting region of FSTs, needed to hold and functionally use an Alphonic distinction. A registered difference is not held by a solitary glyph; its code requires a convention, its measurement use a reference, calibration and uncertainty account, its linguistic use prior trajectories. The framing asks for minimum sufficient accessible account for a given continuation, how that account changes across handlers or resolutions, and which supporting relationships can be compressed while the named operation remains reconstructable. Development questions for this framing are listed in Appendix E.

### Chapter 11 — Bridges Between Mathematical Fields

A bridge permits a relationship established in one representation or field to support work in another. The audit names source, target, mapping and required preservation, then asks whether the intended result needs recovery of a value, a canonical presentation, an original carrier or a particular history. The diagram Φ(*T_A*(Γ_*A*)) |_C *T_B*(Φ(Γ_*A*)) (Eq. 11.1) identifies the required correspondence. Mathematical bridges and empirical crossings have separately declared roles. Parallel FSTs can correspond at relevant nodes and continuations without their entire histories becoming identical, making feedforward through the wider symbolic lattice available as a constructive account of mathematical development.

### Chapter 12 — Proof as a Compound Functional Symbolic Trajectory

A proof contains more than its concluding statement: definitions establish available expressions, lemmas compress earlier routes, substitutions move relations, cited theorems permit steps. A finite proof report can be inspected as a compound FST. Proof dynamics are examined: different proofs of the same result preserve different intermediate constructions and make different later problems accessible. The transition notation *s_i* →^{*r_i*, *C_i*} *s_{i+1}* (Eq. 12.1) records invoked rule and relevant conditions. The audit distinguishes a cited dependency (sufficient for the present task) from a dependency that must be reopened, and records the probe's stopping point.

### Chapter 13 — Order Is Not Dimension

Within the FSM account the carrying lattice has finite extent in the ground three-dimensional setting. Higher-order indexing organises relationships that are realised through inscriptions, stored states and time-extended operations. A matrix organises entries for operations; its printed arrangement makes some relations quickly readable while its actual use unfolds through access, stored state and transformations. The chapter supplies a geometrical discipline for the subsequent object programme: index order, representational organisation and measured spatial extent remain explicitly related through construction. A matrix-vector multiplication trace (Eq. 13.2) displays the access and intermediate sums.

### Chapter 14 — The Functional in FST

The functional in FST cannot be reserved for a final declaration that a claim is physically measurable. Poetry, fiction and nonsense can produce and redirect trajectories. A gold bar can be held as a mass, an asset, a reference, a chemical specimen, an heirloom or a danger: each telling selects and carries relationships that enable particular actions. The audit distinguishes four questions: internal coherence, functional adequacy, exogenous tethering, and measurement admissibility. These questions can interact: a scientific report may require all four, a mathematical proof the first two. The audit's own function is self-limiting: a longer report is not automatically a better account.

### Chapter 15 — From Fundamental to Functional

The mountain image is made precise: a registered difference becomes a coded value, a transformed relation, a named object, a component of a theory — and the object is then used to organise new measurements and explanations. The Greene–Pascal framing asks which part of the producing trajectory is still available when reuse occurs. Refinement uses resources within a system that must continue; continuance remains an exploratory organising idea rather than a universal scalar purpose. Part III carries these links as questions for broader audits.

### Chapter 16 — A General Geofinite Audit

Presents the complete general audit method in five stages: (1) Provenance and purpose — name the construction and the action it must support; (2) Locate and unfold — identify carrier, distinction conditions, uncertainty, accessible history and licence; (3) Inspect licences and correspondences — at formal crossings, at empirical crossings, identify what is required; (4) Witness insufficiency or establish bounded support — using the Part II witness val_C(*L_A*) = val_C(*L_B*) = *v* while Adm_C(*T_{a,C}*, *L_A*) ≠ Adm_C(*T_{a,C}*, *L_B*) (Eqs. 16.1–16.2); (5) Record debt, cost and continuation — name the missing distinction, its last accessible stage and a possible recovery route. The method can be applied to a paradox, a scientific noun, a statistical claim or an LLM-produced proposal.

### Chapter 17 — The Audit Outcome as a Finite Report

The audit's outcome is itself a coherent finite document. It records function as well as limits: a formal construction can be internally usable, an implementation can support a bounded operation, and a measurement bridge can be recurrent under named conditions. Different states of missing information are distinguished: an omitted field may remain in a source report; a displaced entry may remain identifiable elsewhere; a distinction below an earlier registration condition may never have been recorded. The audit closes with recompression: the report can be shortened to a compact card carrying scope, accessible dependencies and the construction's next continuation.

### Chapter 18 — Worked Audit I: Infinity

Specimen: written inductive-set assertion together with a finite successor trace *n*_0 = ∅, *n*_1 = {∅}, *n*_2 = {∅, {∅}} (Eq. 18.1). First purpose — make a classical permission available for a formal argument. Second proposed purpose — authorise a particular next step in a finite construction. A stipulated three-place finite register cannot store the fourth stage; the debt is specific (a fourth accessible stage location under the no-overwrite requirement). The case does not identify Greene–Pascal with the three-place limit; it provides one bounded example of support needed for a continuation.

### Chapter 19 — Worked Audit II: A Set and Its Construction

Specimen: two ledgers *L_A*: *a* → *b*, *L_B*: *b* → *a* (Eq. 19.1) producing the same unordered membership presentation *V*(*L_A*) = *V*(*L_B*) = {*a*, *b*} (Eq. 19.2). A rollback operation *U* yields *V*(*U*(*L_A*)) = {*a*} and *V*(*U*(*L_B*)) = {*b*} (Eq. 19.3): the second form of Part II's insufficiency witness. A reader supplied only {*a*, *b*} cannot determine which result the named rollback must produce. The case closes with two functions and two scopes: membership is supported by the compact set presentation; rollback requires an ordering distinction retained in a richer report.

### Chapter 20 — Worked Audit III: A Polynomial Bridge and Proof

Specimen: the ring isomorphism Φ between finite non-empty coefficient lists and formal polynomials *p* = Σ*a_i X^i* (Eq. 20.1), with Φ(Ψ(*p*)) = *p*, Ψ(Φ(*a*)) = can(*a*) (Eq. 20.2). A finite product (*a* = (1,2), *b* = (3,−1)) is computed via finite convolution (Eq. 20.4) and verified as (1 + 2*X*)(3 − *X*) = 3 + 5*X* − 2*X*² (Eq. 20.5). The preservation proof (Eq. 20.6) is unfolded. The distinction left outside the polynomial: two raw records *r_A* = (1,2) and *r_B* = (1,2,0) share the same polynomial output (Eq. 20.8) but differ in allocated record length — the bridge preserves formal multiplication while leaving the storage continuation insufficient.

### Chapter 21 — Worked Audit IV: A Registered Partition

Develops Part II's partition account into an empirical bridge scenario: *L*: *W* → (*R*, *X*; *h*) (Eq. 21.1). A numerical ratio *v* = ℓ_R/ℓ_W (Eq. 21.2) is a formal operation on supplied values; its empirical use depends on registration conditions and comparison criterion. Retains Part II's eight-unit whole with two ledger paths *L_A* (unit-bead selection) and *L_B* (double-unit bead selection), each yielding one quarter — but only *L_A* admits a further halving under the same whole-bead rule (Eq. 21.3). The spectral continuation from Part II is revisited: partition and spectral cases share an audit architecture while their physical trajectories remain distinct.

### Chapter 22 — The Audit Audits Itself

The language of the volume performs the same operations it inspects: FST, bead, licence, bridge, Alphonic and finite report are themselves compressed trajectories. A terminology ledger records established use, source trajectory and present extension. Greene–Pascal is explicitly provisional — its use points to representational support for holding an Alphonic distinction; a later minimum-chain theorem, cost rule or empirical relation would require additional construction. Working with a language model is addressed: a fluent continuation can introduce a changed meaning while retaining familiar words; recording the distinction reduces the risk. Self-audit preserves the dynamic character of FSM: its next continuation remains available.

### Conclusion — Unfold, Audit and Carry Forward

Part I followed how a representation becomes available; Part II how a finite report carries an account and how extracted values can become insufficient; Part III has followed inherited symbolic constructions through those relationships. ZFC specimens show a differentiated architecture of permissions. The worked cases make the method operational. These findings are finite and local. The Greene–Pascal framing connects distinction to the trajectories needed to hold and use it; its development can proceed through questions of minimum sufficient support, handler dependence, compression and cost. The separate Mathematical Objects programme can now receive a constructive handover: a proposed FSM object should disclose its carrier, construction, operative distinctions, uncertainty, accessible provenance, licence, transformations and permitted continuations.

## Working Vocabulary (Terminology and Notation Ledger)

| Term | Working definition |
|---|---|
| Functional Symbolic Trajectory (FST) | A finite pathway of symbolic states and transformations through which constraints, uncertainty, meaning and admissibility can be carried |
| Finite report | A bounded, instantiated coherent document gathering one or more FSTs and their admitted connections for a declared purpose |
| Ledger | An accessible finite record of relevant actions and distinctions; selective, does not claim a completed history |
| Reported value | A particular extraction from a ledger or report under a rule; compactness can support mobility; sufficiency depends on continuation |
| Bead | An admitted finite distinction that can occupy a position in a symbolic chain and condition an operation |
| Alphonic distinction | The operative condition at which a relevant difference becomes non-single in a registration or representation |
| Greene–Pascal | Provisional framing of the FST or supporting region needed to hold and functionally use an Alphonic distinction |
| Grand Corpus | Pointer to the wider accumulated and continuing field of symbolic trajectories through which local expressions are sustained |
| Handler | The current endogenous lattice through which a representation is reconstructed, compared and incorporated into a continuation |
| Licence | Commitments and permissions under which a construction or transition is admitted; can include axioms, encoding conventions and operational protocols |
| Bridge | A declared correspondence between constructions or trajectories; direction, preservation and return operations belong to its account |
| Conditional insufficiency | A witnessed failure of a compact representation to support a named continuation under declared conditions |
| Representational debt | The particular distinction or record owed by a claim when the presented support does not carry it |
| Representation cost | Declared resources used to produce, carry, inspect, recover or refine support; quantities with different units remain separate |
| Symbolic drag | The residual effect of an earlier trajectory and its commitments upon a compressed expression in a later use |
| Conditioned correspondence | A comparison under stated licence, distinction, uncertainty and history conditions, written with the interactional bar |
