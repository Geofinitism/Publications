## Central Proposition

> "The transform is not applied to the trajectory. The transform is another trajectory."

A matrix is a **finite symbolic inscription** capable of initiating computational unfoldings — not a timeless completed object. No matrix calculation occurs as a single simultaneous gesture. Every entry must be finitely represented, every operation ordered, every result arising through a sequence of computational states. The DCT coefficient is the terminal registration of an ordered sequence of products and accumulations. The SVD is an algorithmic trajectory of finite rotations, reflections, and reductions. The matrix object on the page is a **stabilised symbolic residue** of repeatable computational trajectories — and equality, invariance, and mathematical objecthood are retained, reinterpreted as compressed, measurable, and reproducible relations with provenance.

The concluding statement of the monograph:

> "The symbol compresses the trajectory. The calculation unfolds the symbol. The result stabilises another symbol. The transform is another trajectory."

## Guide for Readers New to FSM: Five Commitments

The monograph opens with an explicit onboarding section for readers encountering Finite Symbolic Mechanics for the first time. Five commitments define the FSM interpretive stance:

1. **Symbols are finite.** Every symbol is a registered mark with finite extent and finite resolution within a given inscription or measurement regime.
2. **Expression ≠ process.** A mathematical expression does not contain the complete process required to produce or use it. Expressions compress trajectories.
3. **Computation proceeds through distinguishable states.** C₀ → C₁ → ... → Cₜ, each transition consuming finite resources, each state fully specifiable.
4. **Registered values are finite distinctions.** Not infinitely precise points: finite symbols represented as spheres with positive radius — uncertainty-bearing, not exact.
5. **Stable mathematical objects are compressed residues of repeatable trajectories.** The matrix is not a timeless object; it is the stabilised, inscribed outcome of a history of procedures.

## Part I — The Historical Formation of the Matrix (Chapters 1–5)

### Chapter 1: Before the Matrix — Coupled Relations and Ordered Procedures

The *fangcheng* procedures of the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* (Ancient China): systems of coupled relations processed by ordered elimination steps. The trajectory E₀ → E₁ → ... → Eₜ was the calculation — the spatial inscription and the temporal operation existed in duality. Determinants preceded matrices: Leibniz's formula det(A) = Σ sgn(π) ∏ aᵢ,π(ᵢ) was computed long before the matrix object existed to hold the coefficients.

### Chapter 2: Naming and Objectification — Sylvester and Cayley

Sylvester introduced the term "matrix" in 1850. Cayley's *Memoir on the Theory of Matrices* (1858) gave the array algebraic standing: y = Ax abbreviated a system; z = By = B(Ax) = (BA)x stabilised as matrix multiplication; inversion, composition, and the characteristic equation followed. **First inversion:** relations and procedures became a matrix object — an autonomous algebraic entity capable of entering into its own relations. The array ceased to be a compressed procedure and became a primary mathematical instrument.

### Chapter 3: The Matrix as a Classical Instrument

Why matrices became indispensable — and the illusion of simultaneous action. Classical notation presents yᵢ = Σ aᵢⱼ xⱼ as if it occurs at once. FSM analysis: s⁽⁰⁾ᵢ = 0, s⁽¹⁾ᵢ = Qᴮ(aᵢ₁x₁), ..., s⁽ⁿ⁾ᵢ = yᵢ,ᴮ — the result requires unfolding. Human calculation required intermediate surfaces. The matrix was a transport instrument, carrying relational structure from one computational episode to another. FSM: matrix formation is a measurement and inscription process.

### Chapter 4: The Matrix Meets the Electronic Computer

**Second inversion:** the matrix object was forced back into finite ordered operations by electronic computation. Rounding entered the foreground: (a + b) + c ≠ a + (b + c) in finite arithmetic. Von Neumann & Goldstine (1947) and Turing (1948, "Rounding-Off Errors in Matrix Processes") confronted finite arithmetic directly. Provenance becomes explicit: (Aᴮ, A, B, p, O, H) — the base, precision, algorithm, and halting condition that produced a numerical result. Memory order (row-major vs column-major) becomes part of the computational identity of a matrix.

### Chapter 5: Factorisation and the Computational Substrate

Factorisation as compressed computational architecture: A = LU, A = QR, A = UΣVᵀ. SVD became computationally tractable — Golub & Kahan (1965): bidiagonalise then diagonalise. BLAS (Lawson et al., 1979): standardised operational vocabulary, creating a feedback loop: matrix mathematics ⇄ computational architecture. The computational substrate is no longer a neutral medium but an active shaper of what counts as a practical matrix operation.

## Part II — Finite Symbolic Foundations (Chapters 6–9)

### Chapter 6: The Finite Symbol and the Inscription

The **Alphonic Limit** α = active lower boundary of distinguishability for a given inscription and measurement regime. Below α, differences cannot be registered. The **Alphonic sphere** S(xᴮ, δ) represents finite extent around a registered value. The four stages of a finite symbolic computation:

> Iᴮ →^G T →^H Rᴮ

Inscription → (grammar/algorithm G) → trajectory T → (halting condition H) → result inscription Rᴮ.

Mathematical object as stabilised residue:

> Oᴮ = Stabilise(T | B, p, H, C)

An object is not a Platonic form — it is the terminal compression of a repeatable trajectory under specified conditions.

### Chapter 7: Mathematical Notation Compresses Temporal Labour

Mathematical notation compresses temporal labour: S = Σxᵢ compresses the sequential accumulation S₀ = 0, S₁ = S₀ + x₁, ..., Sₙ. The inscription-to-trajectory-to-inscription cycle:

> trajectory → inscription → trajectory → inscription

Compression removes provenance: the reader who reads S cannot recover the sequence without re-executing it. The reader is part of the reconstruction of compressed trajectories. Notation is not neutral representation — it is active compression that determines what labour is made visible and what is hidden.

### Chapter 8: Finite Computational States and Computational Time

The **finite computational state**: Cₜ = (Rₜ, Mₜ, Iₜ, Pₜ, Eₜ) — registers, memory, current instruction, partial results, accumulated discrepancy. Observable scalar: sₜ = h(Cₜ). Multiple computational clocks (operation count, loop count, sweep count, memory writes, wall-clock time, checkpoints) produce a partial order on computational events, not a simple linear sequence. Finite transitions: Δₜ = dᴮ(Cₜ₊₁, Cₜ). Serialisation imposes order; parallelism introduces partial order.

### Chapter 9: From Exact Points to Finite Spheres

The exact point zₜ ∈ ℝᵐ is replaced by the finite sphere:

> Zₜ = {z : ||z − ẑₜ|| ≤ rₜ}

A finite computational trajectory is "a temporally ordered necklace of uncertainty-bearing symbolic spheres":

> Z₀ → Z₁ → ... → Zₜ

Sphere overlap: Zₜ ∩ Zₜ₊₁ ≠ ∅ = not yet crossed a finite boundary; Zₜ ∩ Zₜ₊₁ = ∅ = crossed a finite boundary. Trajectory geometry: thickness (sphere radius), folding (trajectory doubles back), projection (high-dimensional trajectory projected to observable coordinates).

## Part III — The Transform as a Finite Trajectory (Chapters 10–12)

### Chapter 10: The Discrete Cosine Transform — Basis, Base, and Execution

Ahmed, Natarajan & Rao (1974). DCT-II classical formula: cₖ = αₖ Σ xₙ cos[π/N(n + ½)k]. Finite execution of one coefficient:

> Dₖ,₀ = 0; Dₖ,ⱼ₊₁ = Qᴮ(Dₖ,ⱼ + Qᴮ(xⱼ Qᴮ(cos θₖⱼ))); cₖ,ᴮ = Qᴮ(αₖ Dₖ,ₙ)

The complete DCT is a **bundle** of trajectories: Tᴅᴄᴛ = {Tᴅᴄᴛ,₀, ..., Tᴅᴄᴛ,ₙ₋₁}. A critical distinction: **basis** (the mathematical structure of cosine functions) ≠ **base** (the finite arithmetic base B in which the computation runs). The DCT is a measurement of recurrence — it measures how much of each recurrent frequency pattern is present in the input. The coefficient is the terminal stabilisation of the DCT execution trajectory, not an immediate read-off of a spectral value.

### Chapter 11: Singular Value Decomposition and the Removal of Privilege

Classical form: A = UΣVᵀ. FSM reading:

> Aᴮ →^{Aₛᵥᴅ/H} (Uᴮ, Σᴮ, Vᵀᴮ)

Finite reconstruction relation (not equality):

> Aᴮ ~ᴮ Uᴮ Σᴮ Vᵀᴮ

The factors are terminal stabilisations — not the causes of the decomposition but its compressed residues. Full provenance: (Uᴮ, Σᴮ, Vᵀᴮ) | (Aᴮ, A, B, p, O, H). The hierarchy: first finite trajectory → matrix inscription → SVD trajectory → terminal factor inscriptions. "The transform is not applied to the trajectory. The transform is another trajectory." The SVD cannot be an external primary operator once the matrix is treated as a finite symbolic inscription.

### Chapter 12: SVD Algorithms as Families of Trajectories

SVD algorithms constitute a family of trajectories: Tₛᵥᴅ = {Tⱼₐꜰᴏᵦᵢ, Tᵦᵢᵈᵢₐɢ, Tᵢₜₑᵣ, Tᵦₗₒꜿₖₑᵈ, ...}. The generic unfolding has ten stages: load → compute norms → construct rotations → update → reduce → repeat sweeps → evaluate residuals → order singular values → apply sign conventions → register. Nested temporal phases: element ⊂ local update ⊂ sweep ⊂ iteration ⊂ halting. Measured stability vs assumed invariance: P(R₁), P(R₂), ... agree within resolution — this is what stability means finitely. Algorithmic equivalence ≠ trajectory equivalence: two algorithms that produce identical singular values may traverse radically different computational paths.

## Part IV — Reconstructing Computational Geometry (Chapters 13–16)

### Chapter 13: From the Computational Line to Delay Coordinates

Takens delay embedding adapted for FSM: zₜ = (sₜ, sₜ₋τ, ..., sₜ₋(ₘ₋₁)τ). FSM uses Takens as a **measurement method**, not a topological guarantee. Sequential temporal difference becomes simultaneous relational coordinate. Finite delay embedding:

> zₜ,ᴮ ↝ Zₜ = S(sₜ) × S(sₜ₋τ) × ... × S(sₜ₋(ₘ₋₁)τ)

Full reconstruction provenance: (h, τ, m, κ, B, p) — observable, delay, embedding dimension, pre-processing, base, precision. A reconstruction is not a window into the true state space; it is another finite symbolic trajectory.

### Chapter 14: Reconstructing DCT and SVD Execution

Observing DCT: z⁽ᴰ⁾ₜ = (dₜ, dₜ₋τ, dₜ₋₂τ) where dₜ tracks accumulator state across coefficient computations. Observing SVD: sₜ records multiple observables simultaneously — ||Aₜ||ᶠ, ||Aₜ − Aₜ₋₁||ᶠ, off-diagonal Frobenius residual, rotation angle, symbolic change count, etc. — and z⁽ˢ⁾ₜ = (sₜ, sₜ₋τ, sₜ₋₂τ). The Hankel matrix Hᵢⱼ = sᵢ₊ⱼ is the classical bridge. Complete trajectory hierarchy: Tₚᵣₒᵦₗₑₘ → Tₘₐₜᵣᵢₓ → Tₜᵣₐₙₛ_ᶠₒᵣₘ → Tᵈₑₗₐᵧ → Tᵥᵢₛᵤₐₗᵢₛₐₜᵢₒₙ → Tᵢₙₜₑᵣₚᵣₑₜₐₜᵢₒₙ.

### Chapter 15: Circular, Toroidal, and Folded Computational Geometry

Computational geometry of delay-reconstructed trajectories:

- **Circular band**: single recurrence, sₜ = A cos(ωt + φ) → S¹
- **Toroidal band**: two coupled phases, sₜ = A₁ cos(ω₁t + φ₁) + A₂ cos(ω₂t + φ₂) → S¹ × S¹
- Nested SVD/DCT phases naturally produce toroidal geometry
- **Folded attractor**: nonlinear recurrence → folded finite structure in delay space

Full geometric hierarchy: terminal state → finite point-like cluster; single recurrence → circle/ellipse; two phases → torus; multiple coupled phases → higher torus; nonlinear recurrence → folded attractor.

### Chapter 16: Recursive Measurement and the Absence of an External Transform

Reconstruction also unfolds: Tₛₒᵤᵣꜿₑ → Tₘₑₐₛᵤᵣₑₘₑₙₜ → Tᵣₑꜿₒₙₛₜᵣᵤꜿₜᵢₒₙ → Tᵈᵢₛₚₗₐᵧ. "A finite symbolic trajectory can measure another trajectory only by producing a further finite symbolic trajectory." There is no view from outside the landscape — the observer is always within the finite symbolic landscape, measuring from a position within it. Halting function H: T → {continue, register} is itself a finite decision procedure. "Delay reconstruction produces a measurable spatial representation of relationships compressed by sequential symbolic presentation."

## Part V — Consequences for Mathematics and Computation (Chapters 17–20)

### Chapter 17: Coefficients, Singular Values, and Terminal Residues

DCT coefficient as terminal residue: cₖ,ᴮ = halt(Tᴅᴄᴛ,ₖ). Singular value as terminal residue: σₖ,ᴮ = halt(Tₛᵥᴅ,ₖ). Residue = stable terminal compression of a trajectory. Ranked residue (truncated SVD): Aₖ = Σⱼ≤ₖ σⱼ uⱼ vᵀⱼ. Compression removes the trajectory twice: once when the matrix inscription compressed the original system of relations, and again when the singular values compressed the SVD trajectory.

### Chapter 18: Equality, Provenance, and Algorithmic Plurality

Four layers of equality:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| = | Formal equality under declared symbolic rules |
| =ᴮ | Identity of finite inscriptions in base B |
| ~ᴮ | Finite reconstruction relation in base B |
| ≈ₚ,ₕ | Agreement under precision p and halting H |

Equality is **reproducibility** — agreement across independent finite trajectories within a stated tolerance and provenance. Algorithmic plurality: A(I) = {A₁, ..., Aₘ} — multiple algorithms producing the same terminal inscription from the same input are not equivalent in trajectory, only in result. Complete result with provenance: R* = (Rᴮ, Π) — terminal inscription plus the complete provenance record that specifies what "same result" means.

### Chapter 19: Measured Complexity and the Geometry of Algorithms

Three measures of trajectory complexity:

**Symbolic path length:** Lᴮ(T) = Σ dᴮ(Cₜ₊₁, Cₜ) — total finite distance traversed through computational state space.

**Overlap density:** ρᴼ = (1/T) Σ oₜ — fraction of consecutive trajectory spheres that overlap (measuring continuity of computation).

**Compression ratio:** Γ = (registered distinctions in trajectory) / (registered distinctions retained in residue) — how much the computation compressed.

Terminal basin stability: whether re-running the trajectory from nearby initial inscriptions produces trajectories that converge to the same terminal sphere.

### Chapter 20: An Experimental Programme

Five experimental programmes for empirical investigation of FSM claims:

1. **Matrix multiplication order**: Direct comparison of different multiplication orders with identical inputs; collect observables; embed with delay coordinates; compare trajectory geometries.
2. **DCT comparison**: Direct vs fast DCT implementation; compare coefficient-by-coefficient trajectories; measure trajectory divergence with identical inputs.
3. **SVD algorithm comparison**: Jacobi iteration vs bidiagonalisation; collect per-sweep observables; reconstruct and compare delay-embedded geometries.
4. **Base and precision variation**: Vary B and p systematically; track how finite arithmetic shapes trajectory geometry and terminal residue.
5. **Reconstruction trajectory**: Instrument the delay embedding procedure itself; observe how the reconstruction trajectory geometry changes with τ, m, and observable choice.
