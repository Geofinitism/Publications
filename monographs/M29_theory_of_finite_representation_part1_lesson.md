**Monograph:** M29 — A Theory of Finite Representation, Part I: Extent, Registration and the Licence to Represent  
**Author:** Kevin R. Haylett  
**Date:** September 2026 (revised draft)  
**Lesson level:** Foundational — introduces the core architecture of the Theory of Finite Representation; no prior FSM formalism required, though familiarity with FSM vocabulary is helpful

---

## How to Use This Lesson

Work through the four sections in order. Each section builds on the previous. Exercises marked with ★ are foundational; those marked with ★★ require more careful engagement with the text and argument; those marked with ★★★ are open-ended and invite original thinking within the frame.

This monograph is a foundational theoretical work in the mode of philosophical-scientific construction. Its arguments unfold through propositions, axioms and principles rather than through empirical data. Engage with each principle by asking: what does it rule out, what does it permit, and what work does it do that could not be done without it?

## Section I: The Forgotten Middle and Three Basins

*Covering the Prologue, Chapters 1–2: The trajectory from interaction to statement; three co-related basins; the bridge; symbolic drag.*

The opening move of M29 is to restore what scientific compression habitually removes: the extended trajectory between a physical interaction and a scientific statement. Before developing the formal architecture, you need a clear grip on what that trajectory is and why the three-basin separation is required.

**Exercise 1 ★**

The monograph opens with Robert Greene's 1727 volume arriving through the post (Prologue). The volume is not interchangeable with its reproductions: its paper has changed colour, marks have been added, and most events in its history cannot be recovered.

(a) What is the difference, in the monograph's terms, between the Greene volume as a *physical carrier* and Greene's *symbolic construction*? What does each carry that the other does not?  
(b) The monograph says: "A representation is never simply present. It becomes extant through a finite carrier, a history of interactions and a community capable of reconstructing its distinctions." Explain each of the three conditions in plain language. What would it mean for a representation to fail one of them while satisfying the other two?  
(c) Why does the monograph use the word *extant* rather than simply *existing* or *available*? What does that word choice do?

**Exercise 2 ★**

Chapter 1 introduces the elementary chain (Eq. 1.1):

> physical interaction → instrumental change → registration → finite symbol → transformation → classification → model → scientific statement

(a) Work through a specific example — choose one of: a spectroscopic observation of a distant star; a blood glucose test; a language model completing a sentence; the registration of a temperature on a digital thermometer. Identify what occupies each arrow in your chosen case.  
(b) The monograph says "every arrow in Equation (1.1) performs work. The arrows are not empty conduits through which an unchanged object passes." For each arrow in your example, state what is changed or what distinctions are created or lost.  
(c) Chapter 1.3 defines *symbolic drag* as "the residual dependence of a compressed symbol upon the path that formed it." Give an example from your chosen case in which symbolic drag would matter — i.e., where knowing the trajectory would change how you interpret or use the final symbol.

**Exercise 3 ★★**

Chapter 2 distinguishes three basins: physical, symbolic and bridge. These are not rigid containers; they are distinguished by their commitments and the kinds of trajectories that can stabilise within them.

(a) Explain in plain language why the theory requires the three basins to be *separated* rather than immediately connected. What goes wrong if properties of one basin are silently transferred to another before a bridge has been declared?  
(b) The bridge basin contains calibration, measurement protocol, model fitting and experimental comparison — but the monograph says "the bridge is not a narrow final step added after physics and mathematics are complete. It operates at many levels." Give two examples of bridge operations occurring at different levels in a single scientific project.  
(c) The three-basin rule states: "No property declared inside one basin crosses into another without a bridge operation. Correspondence may be strong, repeatable and productive without being converted into identity." Construct a scenario (real or hypothetical) in which a practitioner silently converts correspondence into identity, and describe what error or confusion this creates.

**Exercise 4 ★★★**

Chapter 1.2 separates three questions that are "often compressed into one":

1. What changed within the physical interaction and apparatus?
2. What was registered from that change?
3. What was inferred, named or modelled from the registration?

The monograph says "an answer to one cannot be silently substituted for an answer to another."

Choose a case from a domain you know well (any science, technology or language-modelling context) and show how all three questions can receive *different* answers for the same situation — that is, construct a case where the physical change, the registered distinction and the modelled inference all have different characters. What does this triple difference reveal about the trajectory? What would be lost if you answered only one of the three questions and treated it as sufficient?

## Section II: Finite Extent, Registration and the Axiom

*Covering Chapters 3–5: The Principle of Finite Extent; the registration boundary; the Axiom of Finite Representation; six senses of the finite; numbers as measurements.*

This section formalises what it means for a representation to be finite. The key move is to show that finitude is not a defect or approximation — it is the condition under which a representation can do anything at all.

**Exercise 5 ★**

Chapter 3 states the Principle of Finite Extent:

> Whatever the unregistered physical world may be, anything entering an empirical scientific trajectory must produce a finitely distinguishable change across a non-zero interactional extent.

(a) What does it mean for a change to be "finitely distinguishable"? Why must it be distinguishable as opposed to merely existing?  
(b) The principle states Δ > 0 (Eq. 3.1) — a non-zero interactional window. The monograph notes this "does not establish an absolute smallest interval." Explain the difference between saying "Δ > 0 for this registration" and saying "there is a universal minimum of physical extent."  
(c) Why does the monograph say finitude must not be equated with crude? Give an example in which a highly precise result is achieved through a registration that is nonetheless finite in all six senses described in §5.3.

**Exercise 6 ★★**

Chapter 4 introduces the registration operation (Eq. 4.1):

> E →^{G_{α,Δ,δ}} s₀

and the full initial registration state (Eq. 4.2):

> R₀ = (k, α, Δ, δ, H, C)

(a) What does each component of R₀ record? Why does ordinary practice store only k (and parts of δ) while the remaining components "do not cease to exist because they were not carried forward"?  
(b) Section 4.3 says: "Registration is not passive copying." The registered state is *relational*: formed by the encounter between an interactional trajectory and a system capable of being changed by it. Contrast this with the view that an instrument is a window through which an unchanged world becomes visible. What specific differences does the relational account make to how you interpret the registered value?  
(c) Section 4.4 says: "The first symbol is already historical." Design a minimal thought experiment in which two nominally identical registrations s₀ differ from one another precisely because their histories H differ, even though their visible values k are the same. What does this show about the role of H in the working representational state?

**Exercise 7 ★**

Chapter 5 states the Axiom of Finite Representation:

> Every instantiated representation has finite extent, finite distinguishability, bounded resolution and a physical or historical provenance.

And the scientific strengthening:

> Any symbol admitted into an empirical scientific trajectory must be finitely instantiated and must arise through, or be traceable to, a finite chain of registrations and declared symbolic transformations.

(a) Section 5.5 says the axiom "governs the crossing" — it does not prohibit classical mathematics from constructing ideal continua within its own licensed basin. Explain in your own words what "governing the crossing" means. What does the axiom permit that an outright rejection of continua would not?  
(b) Section 5.4 treats numbers within measurement as registered symbolic values rather than imperfect approximations to infinitely exact numbers. What changes in how you interpret a measurement value when you adopt this view? Give a concrete example.  
(c) Chapter 5.3 lists six senses in which the word *finite* is used within the axiom. A representation could be finite in some senses and not others. Construct an example (real or hypothetical) in which a representation is finite in three of the six senses but not all of them, and explain which senses are absent and why that matters.

**Exercise 8 ★★★**

The Axiom of Finite Representation is described as applying to *any crossing by which a mathematical construction acquires empirical application*. This is a narrower claim than saying "all mathematics must be finite."

(a) State precisely what the axiom does and does not restrict. Why is the restriction placed at the crossing rather than inside the mathematical basin?  
(b) Consider a physical theory that uses a continuous differential equation to model a real system. According to the monograph, this is admissible — but what conditions must hold for the model's empirical application to remain within the axiom? What would constitute a violation?  
(c) The monograph suggests that the axiom's real work is conceptual: it prevents the internal elegance of a model from silently constituting proof of its physical claims. Describe a case in which this confusion has actually occurred in any domain (physics, statistics, language modelling, or another field), and explain what a finite-representation audit of that case would reveal.

## Section III: Conditions, Trajectories and the Bridge

*Covering Chapters 6–9: The working representational state; Alphonic distinction; FSTs; the Bridge Axiom; equality, identity and licence.*

Having established the axiom, Part I now builds the full working architecture: the five conditions of representation, the formal structure of symbolic trajectories, the two-directional bridge and the treatment of equality.

**Exercise 9 ★**

Chapter 6 assembles the working representational state (Eq. 6.1):

> R = (s, α, δ, H, C)

where s is the present finite symbol, α the distinction condition, δ its uncertainty, H its accessible history and C the conditions of admissibility.

(a) Chapter 6.6 says: "The usable scientific symbol is not s alone but the conditioned state R = (s, α, δ, H, C). Compression may hide the other components; it does not abolish them." Give a specific example in which two different scientific claims share the same visible value s but differ in one or more of the remaining components. What practical consequence follows from treating the two claims as equivalent because their s values match?  
(b) The monograph distinguishes *admissibility* from *physical correctness*: "Consensus is not a guarantee that a representation is physically correct. It is the stabilising mechanism through which symbols become usable across a community." Why is this distinction important? What would it mean to challenge a consensus claim, within the framework?  
(c) Section 6.3 defines the Alphonic condition α(A, C): the operative boundary at which a relevant difference is registered as non-single. Two physical variations that fall within the same admitted state are *symbolically single* for that operation. Explain why the Alphonic condition is apparatus-dependent and context-dependent rather than a fixed physical limit.

**Exercise 10 ★★**

Chapter 7 defines a Functional Symbolic Trajectory (FST) (Eq. 7.1):

> s₀ →^{f₁} s₁ →^{f₂} s₂ →^{f₃} … →^{f_n} s_n

where each f_i is an admissible transformation under conditions C_i.

(a) Chapter 7.3 says: "The reader becomes an author." An FST does not move intact from writer to reader; the reader receives a finite sequence of marks and reconstructs relations through a previously formed linguistic and experiential basin. Contrast reading-as-reconstruction with the view that reading is a process of decoding a message already complete in the text.  
(b) Chapter 7.4 introduces *trajectory loss*: when many registrations are gathered into a distribution, much of their individual history is compressed. States remain as values while the longer paths that produced them become unrecoverable. Give a specific example of trajectory loss in a statistical or modelling context, and explain what the lost trajectory would have contributed to the interpretation of the endpoint.  
(c) Chapter 7.5 says symbolic drag is not uniform: some prior constraints fade while others dominate the new basin. Design a minimal experiment (in any domain) that could distinguish between a compressed endpoint where the drag is still operative and one where it has substantially faded.

**Exercise 11 ★★**

Chapter 8 states the Bridge Axiom:

> No endogenous mathematical or linguistic construction acquires physical identity solely from its internal coherence. Its empirical application requires a declared correspondence with finite registrations under stated conditions of distinction, uncertainty and provenance.

(a) Explain what "internal coherence" means and why it is necessary but not sufficient for a model to acquire physical identity. Give an example of a mathematically coherent construction that has not (yet) been connected to a registration trajectory.  
(b) The inward crossing (Eq. 8.1): E →^{G} s₀ →^{F_H} s_n →^{L_C} M. The outward crossing (Eq. 8.2): M →^{P_C} R̂ |_C R_new. The interactional bar in the outward crossing means the comparison is conditioned, not an identity. Why is this bar essential? What would it mean to replace the bar with an equals sign in the outward crossing?  
(c) Chapter 8.4 lists several dimensions of bridge strength: recurrence, independence, transportability, discriminating power, provenance-richness. Assess the bridge strength of a scientific correspondence you are familiar with, using at least three of these dimensions. On which dimension is it weakest, and what would improve it?

**Exercise 12 ★★★**

Chapter 9 distinguishes formal equality (x₁ = x₂, Eq. 9.1) from measured correspondence (x₁ |_C x₂, Eq. 9.2) and measured equivalence (R₁ |_{C*} R₂, Eq. 9.4).

(a) Formal equality is a *licensed removal of distinction* within a symbolic basin. Measured equivalence is *conditioned correspondence* between finite trajectories. Write out precisely what is being said in each case, and explain why the two cannot be silently exchanged.  
(b) The monograph says a proof can be unfolded as a finite symbolic trajectory (§13.2). How does treating a proof as an FST change the status of the equals signs that appear within it? Are those equals signs formal equality, measured equivalence, or something else?  
(c) Chapter 9.5 says equality can be read as a "particularly strong compression: two previously distinguishable symbolic paths are licensed to occupy one operative position." Describe a case in which this compressive strength of an equality is both productive (it opens further work) and potentially misleading (it conceals a trajectory that later becomes important). How would the framework ask you to handle this double character of equality?

## Section IV: Compression, Decompression and the Extant Object

*Covering Chapters 10–13 and the Epilogue: The bead-chain model; reading through a handler; how objects become extant; the provisional theory; directions of travel.*

The final section develops the compression–decompression dynamics, shows how scientific objects become extant through stable compression, assembles the provisional theory, and surveys the directions Part II will follow.

**Exercise 13 ★**

Chapter 10 introduces compression (Eq. 10.2):

> (b₁, b₂, …, b_n) →^{κ_{Λ,C}} β

and decompression (Eq. 10.5):

> β →^{D_{Λ_t,C}} B̂_t = (b̂₁, b̂₂, …, b̂_q)

where the hat over B̂ is essential: the reconstructed chain need not be identical to the trajectory originally compressed.

(a) Explain the difference between a lossless codec (where compression and decompression recover the original chain exactly) and the kind of compression described here. Why does the monograph say that linguistic and scientific decompression are usually of the second kind?  
(b) Equation 10.6 states that two readers with different handlers Λ_a and Λ_b can decompress the same word into related but non-identical chains: D_{Λ_a,C}(β) ≠ D_{Λ_b,C}(β). The monograph says communication remains possible because language stabilises enough shared constraints for the chains to correspond. Design a scenario in which this shared constraint begins to break down — where the decompressions diverge enough to produce practical misunderstanding. What would restore convergence?  
(c) The reading cycle (Eq. 10.8): register → decompress → compare → construct → reinject. Explain why the reading cycle does not end with decompression — why comparison, construction and reinjection are necessary further steps.

**Exercise 14 ★★**

Chapter 10.4 introduces reading through a handler: the endogenous lattice Λ_t is the present configuration of learned distinctions, active context, remembered trajectories, expectations and already constructed beads. The handler at t + 1 is not identical to the handler at t.

(a) A single reader reads the same page twice at an interval of several years. According to the monograph, what has changed between the two readings even if the page is physically unchanged? What does this imply about the status of "the meaning of the text"?  
(b) The monograph draws on language models as an instance of this framework (§13.6): attention can be examined as a form of relational delay embedding, and a Takens-based transformer can explore whether trajectory reconstruction offers an alternative architecture. How does treating a language model as a history-dependent handler — rather than a text-completion engine — change how you would evaluate its outputs?  
(c) Chapter 10.5 extends the compression hierarchy to multiple scales (Eq. 10.10): phoneme chain → word bead → phrase bead → proposition bead → object bead → theory bead. Identify where in this hierarchy a particular scientific noun you are familiar with sits, and trace at least two levels of compression that produced it. What trajectory is concealed in the compression at each level?

**Exercise 15 ★★**

Chapter 11 asks how scientific objects become extant. The answer is through stable compression:

> {r₁, r₂, …, r_n} →^{F_H} feature →^{L_C} object

An object becomes scientifically extant when a stable symbolic compression can be reconstructed from admitted registrations and can productively constrain future symbolic or physical operations.

(a) Chapter 11.2 distinguishes the *scientific object* (tethered to exogenous registrations through declared bridges) from the *flying pig* (stabilised within a symbolic basin through narrative coherence and shared language). The monograph says: "Both processes share symbolic construction but have different bridge structures." Is this distinction equivalent to the distinction between real and imaginary objects? Why or why not?  
(b) Chapter 11.4 says: "The continuity of spelling does not guarantee continuity of meaning." Give an example of a scientific noun that has been used at two different scales, in two different regimes, or by two different communities — where the noun is spelled the same but the compression it performs (and therefore its bridge structure) has shifted significantly.  
(c) The "becoming extant" condition requires both that the compression can be reconstructed from admitted registrations *and* that it can productively constrain future operations. Why are both conditions required? Give an example of a compression that satisfies the first condition but not the second, and one that would satisfy the second but not the first (if this is coherent).

**Exercise 16 ★★★**

Chapter 12 assembles seven working principles. Consider the Recoverable Decompression Principle:

> Critical analysis proceeds by decompressing symbolic beads towards their registrations, transformations, uncertainties, provenance and bridge conditions. The reconstruction will always be partial and handler-dependent, but its limits should be declared rather than hidden.

(a) "Partial and handler-dependent" — explain both qualifiers. How does a decompression remain useful if it is partial? What does it mean to *declare* the limits of a reconstruction rather than hiding them?  
(b) Chapter 12.4 says the theory "is most useful when it asks whether a move is admissible under declared conditions, not when it issues universal prohibitions." Construct a case in which admissibility-under-declared-conditions gives a more useful answer than an outright prohibition, and one in which the prohibition might still be appropriate.  
(c) Chapter 12.5 says Part I "does not prove that all continua are physically false." Explain precisely what the theory *does* claim about continua, and what it leaves open. Why does the monograph treat these absences as generative rather than as failures?

**Exercise 17 ★★**

Chapter 13 surveys seven directions of travel — language, mathematics and proof, instruments, images, statistics, artificial intelligence, and the programme of Part II. Select any two and, for each one:

(a) State in your own words what specific question the Theory of Finite Representation opens up in that domain that could not be asked (or would be less clearly formulated) from a standard perspective in that domain.  
(b) Identify one concrete research question or project that the direction points towards.  
(c) Explain what the three-basin separation would contribute to work in that direction — i.e., why it matters whether physical, symbolic and bridge operations are distinguished in that domain.

**Exercise 18 ★★★**

The Epilogue closes with the observation that a finite representation "creates a bounded and historically situated place from which another trajectory can begin."

Design a *finite representation audit* — a structured procedure that could be applied to any scientific claim or model to assess its standing within the theory. Your audit should:

(a) Specify what questions must be answered about the claim's *registration chain*: where physical interaction enters the trajectory, what the Alphonic conditions were, what the interactional windows and uncertainty records are, and what has been lost in the compression to the current form.  
(b) Specify what questions must be answered about the claim's *bridge structure*: what the inward and outward crossings are, what dimensions of bridge strength have been assessed, and whether the interactional bar has been properly maintained (i.e., correspondence has not been silently converted to identity).  
(c) Specify what questions must be answered about the claim's *symbolic trajectory*: what compressions have been performed, what the handler-dependence of the key decompression steps is, where symbolic drag is likely to be operative, and whether the seven working principles are satisfied.  
(d) Apply your audit to one specific claim, result or model you are familiar with. What does the audit reveal that would not be visible from a standard methodological review?  
(e) Identify the one step of your audit that is most difficult to complete in practice, and propose what kind of tool, practice or institutional commitment would make it more feasible.
