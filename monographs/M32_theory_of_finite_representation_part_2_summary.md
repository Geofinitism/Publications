# A Theory of Finite Representation, Part II: Finite Reports, Accounts and Operational Insufficiency

**Author:** Kevin R. Haylett  
**Series:** A Theory of Finite Representation (Part II)  
**Date:** First working draft, 26 September 2026  
**Pages:** 29 (content) + vi (front matter)

---

## Abstract

An arithmetic result can be correct under its rules and insufficient as an account of a finite action. Subtracting one half from a whole yields one half as a value, but that remainder does not name the disposition of the part removed. Multiplying two halves yields one quarter, but distinct finite cutting ledgers can yield that same value and support different subsequent cuts. This volume develops these examples into a theory of finite reports. A finite report is a bounded, instantiated formal document that assembles one or more Functional Symbolic Trajectories into a coherent structure for a declared purpose. A reported value is a smaller symbolic result extracted from such a structure. The distinction prevents the word *report* from being compressed into one numeral.

The proposed account distinguishes the bead path, which follows admitted finite distinctions and actions; the ledger path, which keeps their order, disposition and provenance; and the classical path, which operates on reported values. Operational insufficiency is witnessed when two admitted finite ledgers yield the same value but a named next action is admissible for only one, or their continuations fail a declared correspondence test. Representational debt names the specific omitted or unavailable distinctions needed by a later claim. Neither insufficiency nor debt is assumed to be an absolute property of a number. The volume follows addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, mathematical proofs and scientific reports, then treats spectral comparison as an opening bridge case. It closes at the threshold of a further study of FSM mathematical objects.

## Prefatory Context

This is the second part of *A Theory of Finite Representation*. Part I (*Extent, Registration and the Licence to Represent*, M29) established the route from interactional trajectory to finite registration, developed the Functional Symbolic Trajectory (FST), and articulated the licences by which compressed symbols become available for further use. Part II asks how such trajectories are assembled into accounts, how a compact value can travel without its account, and when the missing distinctions become consequential for the next operation.

The draft note explicitly frames this as a *working construction*: the finite account, conditional insufficiency witness and debt register are offered as provisional instruments of thought. Classical equalities retain their exact meaning within the classical symbolic basin. The central examples are deliberately elementary — a half in subtraction, a quarter in multiplication, a ledger that supports one further cut but not another. The reader need not accept a proposed physics of light to inspect the fate of a half in subtraction.

The appendix *Prior Explorations, September 2026* preserves the route through which the draft was formed, including the working document *Finite Accounts and the Discarded Half* (cited as [2]) that first made the second column visible.

## Chapter Summaries

### Prologue: The other half

Opens with the equation 1 − ½ = ½ and distinguishes two questions it can answer and one it cannot. The value ½ is correct under arithmetic rules; but which half remained, which half was set aside, and what record permits either to be identified again? The right side does not answer. The distinction, once made, generalises: an account of a vault transfer must hold the party, destination, division procedure, uncertainty and residue alongside the numerical remainder. The prologue frames the book's central question: the difference between the right answer and the adequate account.

### Chapter 1: From finite representation to finite reports

Recalls Part I's three-basin architecture (interactional, endogenous symbolic, and bridge) and the FST as the unit that carries accounts through compression and decompression. Part II asks what happens when several FSTs must support a decision together, especially when one of their values is detached and sent onward alone. A *finite report* is defined as a bounded, instantiated formal document:

$$\mathcal{F}_C = (\Gamma_1, \ldots, \Gamma_m;\, J, H, C), \quad m < \infty,$$

where each Γ_i is an accessible finite trajectory, J records admitted connections, H records accessible provenance, and C identifies the report's licence and scope. A *reported value* val_C(L) = v is a particular extraction from a ledger or report — smaller than the document that supports it, and misleading to conflate with the report itself. A report closes only with respect to a stated task and remains an available bead for further inquiry.

### Chapter 2: Beads, ledgers and the classical path

Introduces the three-path account of a single calculation. The *bead path* attends to the particular registered distinctions presented and acts performed on them. The *ledger path* records their order, what was retained, what moved aside and which distinctions remained available. The *classical path* manipulates values under a specified arithmetic rule. The paths can correspond; they need not be the same trajectory.

For a bounded exercise, ledger evolution is written:

$$L_n \xrightarrow{T_{a,C}} L_{n+1}, \qquad \mathrm{val}_C(L_n) = v_n,$$

where T_{a,C} may fail to be admitted for a particular L_n. The bridge question — the relation between val_C(L_{n+1}) and the arithmetic result f_a(v_n) — cannot be settled by replacing the ledger with the value before the action has been examined. Ledger selectivity: a ledger is not a complete transcript of the world. Its function is to make the basis of a continuation inspectable. Its length is a cost as well as a resource; the report must say which cost it has chosen to carry.

### Chapter 3: Addition and the first compression

Addition is the first clear constructive story: two chains joined, counted under a common rule, reported as five. The classical equality 2 + 3 = 5 does not require the particular beads to retain names. But the order of joining may matter to a later action even when it does not affect the count. Two distinct ledger trajectories can yield the same reported value (e.g. 1 + 4 = 2 + 3 = 5) without claiming any difficulty in the equality of the results. Addition compresses assembly under its rules; the specific ledger may become relevant when the result is asked to support a further action. The chapter closes by noting that the meaning of multiplication by a fraction (repeat the addition half a time) does not describe the same admitted finite procedure as repeated addition of whole units — the constructive route changes even when the numerical sign stays stable.

### Chapter 4: Subtraction and the contra-entry

Returns to 1 − ½ = ½ and gives a partition account:

$$W \longmapsto (R, X;\, h), \qquad \mathrm{val}_C(W) = \mathrm{val}_C(R) + \mathrm{val}_C(X),$$

where W is the registered whole, R the retained portion, X the portion set aside and h the accessible event record. The classical value on the right is a value relation under C; it does not identify the histories or carriers of W, R and X. If the operation involves a kerf, loss in handling or an uncertain comparison, the account must add those distinctions rather than forcing the equation to carry more than its licence supplies. The debt arises when a later claim needs an entry the travelling result does not contain — if a vault report says only that half the gold remains, it is insufficient for a question about custody of the other half. The older double-entry image is productive precisely here: the second column is not a metaphysical duplicate but a place in the finite document where a transfer can be recorded.

### Chapter 5: Multiplication, cutting and scaling

A fraction can be read as a value and, under a declared whole, as an instruction for an action. The instruction ½ says to partition an admitted extent and retain a portion corresponding to one of two equal shares. Two successive halvings compress into the proportion ¼:

$$\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}. \tag{5.1}$$

The arithmetic scale S_r(v) = rv is defined within its formal domain. A finite cutting operation T_{r,C}(L) has a narrower domain and an event record. The same written r can thus occupy the role of value, ratio or instruction; its role should be declared whenever the distinction matters.

The eight-unit construction (§5.2) exhibits two ledgers L_A and L_B reaching the same value val_C(L_A) = val_C(L_B) = ¼ through distinct bead compositions. A further halving under the same rule is admissible on L_A but halts on L_B (which contains one two-unit bead that the rule does not admit dividing). This is the first *finite witness*: the reported quarter is sufficient for comparisons depending only on its numerical proportion, but insufficient to decide the admissibility of the next cut under this finite rule. The chapter establishes a local result, not a universal prohibition on fraction multiplication.

### Chapter 6: Division, decimal writing and the chosen unit

Division can partition a whole into equal portions, ask how many portions of a given size can be formed, calculate a ratio, or produce a quotient and remainder. The symbol ÷ gathers different permitted procedures in the classical basin; a finite report of a particular procedure may need to state its reference whole, resolution and remainder. The decimal 0.5 makes a half available through a base-ten inscription — one may write 5/10 and, under a permitted reduction, 1/2. The inscription shows an internal place-value construction but does not identify a unique cutting history. A change of numeral base changes the written bead chain and the cost of carrying it; it does not, by mere re-expression, change the value of the ratio.

The two-unit bead from the earlier specimen makes the issue concrete: classical division by two yields one extent unit, but the stipulated operation on a single registered two-unit bead has no admitted half-bead state — the proposed cut is *insufficiently supported at this resolution*, not that one is an impossible number. A measured number must consequently be more than an exact real value with an ornamental error sign attached; its reference, finite inscription, available distinction, uncertainty, provenance and admitted use belong to its account.

### Chapter 7: Conditional sufficiency

Defines operational insufficiency more formally. Let a be a particular proposed action and C the conditions under which a ledger and its reported value are being used. Two exhibited ledgers L_A and L_B constitute an *operational insufficiency witness* for the extracted value v if:

$$\mathrm{val}_C(L_A) = \mathrm{val}_C(L_B) = v, \qquad \mathrm{Adm}_C(T_{a,C}, L_A) \neq \mathrm{Adm}_C(T_{a,C}, L_B). \tag{7.1}$$

A second form of witness occurs if both continuations are admitted but their resulting registrations fail a correspondence required for the particular claim. The quarter-bead story witnesses precisely this situation. Insufficiency belongs to a stated operation and purpose, not to the glyph ¼ in isolation.

The other direction (§7.2): a compact value is sufficient within a declared scope if every ledger in an examined family supports the relevant continuation and the resulting extracted values meet the declared correspondence test. The scope is part of the result. A report that omits this scope can make a local achievement appear to be an unlimited licence.

§7.3 poses a theorem-shaped question: for a declared family of finite ledgers, can one identify the smallest accessible record needed to decide a named continuation? The task of Part II is to make these proof obligations speakable before claiming an exhaustive calculus of FSM objects.

### Chapter 8: Representational debt and recoverability

Debt is an accounting relation, not an extra substance. A debt becomes relevant when a subsequent claim relies on a distinction omitted from the carrier presented to support it. A debt entry should name: the distinction required, the stage at which it was last accessible, its present status, and the proposed action or claim needing it. This is an ordered ledger of obligations: some entries concern a set-aside bead still available in another column; some concern a provenance field present in an earlier file but absent from the figure cited; some concern a registration never made and cannot be discharged by a more confident restatement of the final value.

Two quarters can agree exactly under a classical valuation and remain different as accessible chains (§8.2). Numerical value uncertainty and uncertainty about provenance need not move together. The Alphonic condition α and uncertainty δ perform different roles: α says which differences were registered as non-single in an act; δ describes the limits of a particular claim formed from such acts.

§8.3 identifies the important future problem of representation cost — longer bead chains require storage, reading, checking and perhaps interactional work; a one-line value compresses that burden. It is tempting to add the missing history into a single debt or density variable, but no common unit has been constructed for displaced material, omitted provenance and symbolic processing cost. Part II keeps the entries separate. Until then, *debt* names what is owed by a particular assertion, and *insufficiency* names a witnessed failure of support.

### Chapter 9: The mathematical report

Mathematical proofs are exemplary finite reports: inscriptions, definitions, lemmas, substitutions and decisions about which prior results may be invoked without unfolding their entire derivations. A report of a theorem is therefore not identical to the theorem regarded as a portable formal conclusion.

§9.2 addresses algebraic cancellation: (a + b) − b = a. The formal rule is licensed for the relevant algebraic objects. The visible b on the right is gone because the form of the answer was reduced; it has not established that a particular material b was annihilated. Cancellation is a *licence to ignore* within a declared symbolic basin. If a later claim about a measured event has silently borrowed its exact cancellation without declaring the bridge, it has incurred a debt.

§9.3: A proof crosses only by a bridge. A theorem established over ideal functions may organise the prediction of a finite measurement. Its internal success does not, by itself, specify the sensor, window, calibration, uncertainty or admissible comparison. To document them is not to diminish the proof. Within the proof, = performs the declared formal identity. At the bridge, |_C reminds us that model and registration correspond under conditions whose finite documentation matters.

### Chapter 10: The scientific report

A scientific report gathers several trajectories: the interactional registration, an instrument's own history, calibration, numerical transformations, exclusions, uncertainty and the argument that makes a new claim. A displayed image can be one of several finite reports built from the same recorded array; choice of exposure, background, scaling, interpolation and colour map can alter the visible distinctions that a later reader makes.

§10.2: The *generonic chain* — ADC, sensor, sampling window, gain, software — all condition the registration. A reported number can be stable even when a different part of that chain would yield a different admission decision for a later task. The scientific report should record which version of a registered trajectory has been carried forward. When an event was below the operative distinction condition, no later multiplication of decimal places can retroactively create its earlier registration.

§10.3: A report can earn a stronger bridge through independent instruments, repeated measurements, standards and alternative reductions that provide overlapping paths constraining an interpretation. The bridge becomes stronger when the same conditioned claim survives these changes and the report makes their relationships inspectable. That strength is not a single eternal number; it belongs to the range over which the comparisons have been made and to the future tests that the report can help design.

### Chapter 11: A spectral comparison as an opening case

Takes the spectral comparison as the first bridge case. Within the classical comparison the familiar parameter is:

$$z = \frac{\lambda_\mathrm{obs} - \lambda_\mathrm{ref}}{\lambda_\mathrm{ref}}. \tag{11.1}$$

The expression is a precise rule on supplied values. The words *observed* and *reference* name positions within reports, not ready-made objects travelling intact through an unobserved interval. Each λ has a registration and calibration history, an uncertainty, a feature-identification decision and an admissibility condition. The compact z does not by itself enumerate them.

§11.2: A line can change its reported position, become wider under a fitting convention, or change its separation from neighbouring features. A report should say which distinction is being compared before it asks how to interpret it. Existing reference resources distinguish observed and derived line positions, attach uncertainties, and retain choices such as air or vacuum wavelength conventions. Survey reduction documentation describes the movement from raw and calibration exposures through masks, noise estimates, co-added spectra and fitted redshifts. A finite-report audit can be carried out against these actual documented stages, rather than inventing a generic pipeline that no observer uses.

§11.3: The spectral case is placed at the end of the volume because it places the arithmetic account under the pressure of a genuine scientific comparison. It is not a claim that representation theory already supplies a quantitative alternative to existing models of light or cosmology. That further claim would require an explicit rule connecting finite registered trajectories to a family of predicted spectral comparisons, varied instruments, and independent checks. The value of this chapter is to give the future experiment a clear question: which distinctions survive when the familiar spectral nouns are unfolded, and what new registration is needed to decide between two admitted continuations?

### Chapter 12: Audit rules and the handover to objects

§12.1 proposes a repeatable audit method: begin with a claim whose proposed use is known; identify the finite report that presently supports it; follow the particular FSTs by which its relevant registrations were formed, transformed and connected; mark the points where a value, noun, fitted feature or mathematical result became a portable bead; ask what the compression retained, what was explicitly moved to another column, what remains accessible in a companion record, and which distinction never entered an available registration; identify the exact next action or bridge the claim asks the bead to support.

§12.2 states the limits of the present formalism: no general scalar measure of representational debt has been derived; no proof that a difference between histories must alter every future measured value. The insufficiency witness is local and conditional. If later work supplies a metric, a theorem of sufficiency over a bounded domain or an instrumentally discriminating alternative model, it must identify the new registrations and conditions that support it.

§12.3 — The object reopened: What does a mathematical object become when understood as a recurrently accessible stabilisation within a symbolic lattice? Which finite continuations does the particular registration license, and where are the limits of its reconstruction recorded? A measured number, finite Summa or embedded transform object could be named only after its construction and admissibility have been made inspectable. Part II therefore ends at the doorway of objecthood rather than attempting to resolve all of its mathematics in one volume.

### Conclusion: A correct answer and an insufficient account

The arithmetic 1 − ½ = ½ remains correct under its rules. An account of a particular taking asks a further question about disposition, history and the registered whole. Likewise, ½ × ½ = ¼ remains a correct value relation, while two finite bead ledgers yielding that value can diverge in the next admissible cut. The point is not that mathematical symbols are empty — they are powerful compressions, and a compression has a range of subsequent uses under a licence. A finite report is a document in which such licences, compressions and histories can be brought together. Insufficiency names that particular need. Debt keeps its required entries visible without declaring them a hidden universal substance. A theory of FSM objects and a physics of particular observations remain trajectories to be built from it.
