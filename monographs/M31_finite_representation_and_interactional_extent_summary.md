# M31 — Finite Representation and Interactional Extent

**Subtitle:** From the prototype metre to the compressed parsec
**Author:** Kevin R. Haylett
**Series:** Foundational Measurement Series
**Date:** 23 September 2026
**Pages:** 29

---

## Abstract

A short inscription can stand for a long chain of measured and inferred relations. The expression "1 m" may refer to an interval set by comparison with a physical standard, a length realised through contemporary metrology, or a spatial array described at a stipulated resolution. These are different trajectories compressed into similar symbols. This monograph constructs a conditional account of their cost under the licence of Finite Symbolic Mechanics (FSM). It distinguishes the cost of an inscription, the information needed to specify a length, the cost of an explicitly resolved representation, and the resources expended in an actual measurement. It introduces a resolution-dependent ladder from millimetre to megaparsec, keeps the Alphonic distinction scale separate from the Greene–Pascal condition for a usable trajectory, and locates comparison remainders at the bridges between registrations and models. Finally it gives a research programme for bringing this account to classical physics, beginning with local optical measurements and proceeding carefully to spectroscopy and astronomical distance inference. The interval counts developed here are conditional properties of a representation. Whether any associated structure changes the propagation of light or the outcomes of other interactions remains an empirical question.

---

## Prefatory Context

This monograph is the third step in a sequence: *A Theory of Finite Representation, Part I* (M29) established the conditions under which a finite symbol can acquire scientific use; *Registration of Extent* (M30) followed an extended physical interaction towards a stable registration and its symbolic expression. M31 takes up the compression at that crossing. Once an inscription such as "1 m" is licensed to stand for an extent, the question becomes: what must be retained to reconstruct its meaning at a declared resolution, and which of those representational costs, if any, enter a physical model?

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1 — A licence for measured expressions (pp. 1–2)

Opens by situating M31 as the third step after M29 (*Theory of Finite Representation I*) and M30 (*Registration of Extent*). The question is: once an inscription like "1 m" is licensed to stand for an extent, what must be retained to reconstruct its meaning at a declared resolution? Introduces the general comparison expression **ℛ_F |_𝓛 Φ(ℛ_m, ℛ_a)** — a licensed comparison or interactional correspondence, not an algebraic equals sign. The remainder κ_𝓛(ℛ_F, Φ(ℛ_m, ℛ_a)) records what the comparison leaves unresolved. A physical equation admits both a formal and a physical reading; FSM requires a finite path through instruments, comparisons, records, and calibrations for the physical claim, and does not automatically make the mathematical identity into an interactional identity among registrations. The older k_ma in finite mechanics expressions made a remainder visible when trajectories were brought together; it did not thereby establish a new force.

### Chapter 2 — The metre as a trajectory (pp. 3–4)

Uses the platinum–iridium international prototype metre as a first case. The metre has been licensed by different routes across history — direct comparison with the prototype bar, an operational specification via radiation wavelength, and the present SI definition through the fixed numerical value of the vacuum speed of light. Each route licenses the compact symbol *m* while changing the provenance and operations by which a length is realised. To record a measured length to a certain precision is one task; to print the unit name is another; to draw a spatially resolved record across the entire extent is a third; to reproduce the prototype by laboratory comparison is a fourth. Their resources need not be equal. Introduces the **coverage count**:

$$N_{\mathrm{cov}}(L, \Delta) = \left\lceil \frac{L}{\Delta} \right\rceil$$

This counts places in one chosen representation under a resolution rule Δ > 0. It asserts neither that space is granular nor that every interval has been separately measured.

### Chapter 3 — Four costs within one symbol (pp. 5–6)

Distinguishes four incompatible senses of "cost" that a single symbol can carry:

1. **Surface cost** C_surf(s; e) — character count or byte length; sensitive to typography and encoding.
2. **Scalar description cost** — bits to specify a length lying between 0 and L_max in bins of width Δ; M = ⌊L_max/Δ⌋ + 1 bin labels requiring at least ⌈log₂ M⌉ bits.
3. **Explicit resolved record** — bN_cov bits if each of the N_cov intervals is labelled with b bits; this is a direct array.
4. **Physical realisation cost** C_phys(P) — detector events, elapsed time, calibration work, heat, computation under a particular experimental protocol P.

These are collected into a **cost vector**:

$$\mathcal{C}(L, \Delta; P, e) = (C_{\mathrm{surf}}, C_{\mathrm{scalar}}, C_{\mathrm{array}}, C_{\mathrm{phys}})_{L,\Delta;P,e}$$

A comparison or ratio is licensed only between like components under a shared task. Printing "1 m" does not execute 10^11 physical operations merely because one later imagines the length partitioned into 10 pm intervals.

### Chapter 4 — Alphonic scale and symbolic usability (pp. 7–8)

*[Chapter heading: "Alphonic distinction and the Greene–Pascal condition"]*

Separates two independent questions about finite symbolic representation:

1. The **Alphonic scale α** names the smallest selected distinguishable resolution in a stated practice (~10–20 pm as an illustrative, protocol-dependent anchor). It is a provisional premise of the examples, not an established universal smallest feature of matter or language.
2. The **Greene–Pascal condition G_GP(P, H, U)** addresses how much finite trajectory must be retained before a mark or chain can function as a usable symbol — a threshold for stable comparison, readable inscription, or communicable measurement. A provisional grid cost per retained interval c_GP gives **C_grid = c_GP N_cov**. This is a stipulated cost model for a required explicit grid, not a deduction that every metre physically contains N_cov independent Greene–Pascal events.

The two scales are kept separate: α may parametrise the smallest selected distinction, while G_GP expresses an admissibility condition upon a trajectory. Setting them equal would erase the distinction the theory needs.

### Chapter 5 — The resolution ladder (pp. 9–10)

Table 5.1 gives conditional coverage counts N_cov across extents from 1 mm to 1 Mpc at resolutions of 1 mm and 10 pm. Representative entries:

| Extent L | L in metres | N_cov at 1 mm | N_cov at 10 pm |
|----------|-------------|---------------|-----------------|
| 1 mm | 10^−3 | 1 | 10^8 |
| 1 m | 1 | 10^3 | 10^11 |
| 1 km | 10^3 | 10^6 | 10^14 |
| 1 au | 1.496×10^11 | 1.496×10^14 | 1.496×10^22 |
| 1 pc | 3.086×10^16 | 3.086×10^19 | 3.086×10^27 |
| 1 Mpc | 3.086×10^22 | 3.086×10^25 | 3.086×10^33 |

These nominal conversions set the ladder but do not imply that every astronomical baseline has been directly measured at 10 pm. The count changes in direct proportion to 1/Δ. A 10 pm grid across one parsec contains roughly 3.086×10^16 times the count across one metre. Describing the upper endpoint of a scalar specification up to one parsec needs only about 92 binary positions; the compression hides a possible explicit reconstruction, not a proven physical expenditure made by the act of saying "parsec".

### Chapter 6 — Interactional extent and overlap (pp. 11–12)

*[Chapter heading: "Finite interactional extents and overlapping registrations"]*

Real instruments have spatial response kernels h_i(x); successive registrations need not observe isolated disjoint intervals. Let r_i be the recorded response and **Σ_ij = Cov(r_i, r_j)** their covariance under a specified ensemble and protocol. A large nominal N_cov may coexist with a much smaller number of recoverable independent distinctions because kernels overlap, signals are smooth, or uncertainty prevents stable separation. Introduces an **overlap parameter ω** and a **weighted ledger cost**:

$$C_{\mathrm{ledger}}(L; P) = \int_0^L c_P(x)\,\rho_P(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

where ρ_P(x) is the density of required registrations per unit extent and c_P the cost per registration. This separates geometrical extent (a label, giving nominal N_cov) from **interactional extent** (what an instrument actually distinguishes through its finite response window). The model may retain idealised instantaneous values as useful mathematical operations, while the FSM account records what was compressed by them.

### Chapter 7 — Chains, compression and remainders (pp. 13–14)

A registration is rarely turned into a physical claim in one move. Introduces the **transformation chain**:

$$B_0 \xrightarrow{T_0, \mathcal{L}_0} B_1 \xrightarrow{T_1, \mathcal{L}_1} \cdots \xrightarrow{T_{n-1}, \mathcal{L}_{n-1}} B_n$$

where each transformation T_j and licence 𝓛_j declares what information survives, what uncertainty is propagated, and what sources of variation are removed or treated as irrelevant. The **remainder κ_j** records the distinction still relevant to the purpose of the comparison but no longer reconstructible from B_{j+1} alone. Multiple remainders should not automatically be added; their dimensions, correlations and locations in the chain may differ. In this sense, the compact sign "1 m" can stand for a calibration history, an uncertainty budget, a unit definition, and an intended resolution through provenance and external practice — not by literally storing those data in the printed characters. Decompression is an activity that uses those resources to construct a longer trajectory.

### Chapter 8 — A measured spectrum and an inferred distance (pp. 15–16)

Applies the framework to spectroscopy and astronomical distance inference as a case study. Spectroscopy provides a chain whose links can be separately examined: received light interacts with optics and detector; detector readings are calibrated; features are identified; positions become estimated wavelengths; observed positions are compared with reference lines; the redshift parameter is reported:

$$z = \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}}$$

This z is a ratio derived from spectral registrations and their calibration. Distance is inferred through a further chain that may draw on parallax, standard candles, other observations, and a cosmological model. The analogy with sound Doppler measurements should be decompressed with care: wave mathematics provides useful relations across sound and electromagnetic cases, but does not prove that the media, trajectories, calibration procedures, or distance inferences are identical. A large value of N_cov(L, Δ) for an inferred astronomical L is not itself evidence of unmodelled spectral displacement — without a bridge rule coupling cost to an observable, representational cost remains a description of records and chosen reconstruction, not a physical prediction.

### Chapter 9 — What would make cost physically consequential? (pp. 17–18)

Articulates what is required to move from representational accounting to physical prediction. The missing bridge is written as a conditional hypothesis:

$$\mathcal{I}_L = \mathcal{M}_0(\mathcal{I}_0, L, \theta) + \mathcal{B}(Q_P(L), \theta_B)$$

where ℳ_0 is a conventional baseline model and ℬ a specified additional mapping. Before it becomes physics, Q_P must be independent of merely changing the scientist's notation: replacing 10 pm with 20 pm in a ledger cannot alter a detector reading unless the altered scale changes an implemented protocol or stands for an independently evidenced property of the interaction. A measured dependence on actual path length, material, aperture, stabilisation, delay, or registration conditions would supply a possible physical route; a count defined solely by L/Δ would not. This yields two coherent levels of enquiry: on the symbolic level, assess what a formal equation compresses, compare histories, and propagate uncertainty; on the physical level, propose a coupling with an operational variable and compare its prediction against measured residuals under the same registration rules.

### Chapter 10 — A programme for classical results (pp. 19–20)

Four experimental programmes for bringing the FSM account to classical and astronomical physics:

1. **Metrological programme** — for one local length, make several records (short unit inscription, scalar estimate, sampled spatial profile) at several resolutions; determine whether the measured number of stable distinctions follows the proposed rule; record response kernels, covariance, and provenance; compare prototype-bar-style chain with modern optical realisation as different means of licensing "one metre".

2. **Optical and local programme** — laser, pinhole and CCD experiment varying actual source–aperture and aperture–detector separations, exposure, pixel aggregation, and optical geometry; construct delay embeddings only after retaining order and timestamps; fit an established diffraction model; ask whether a reproducible remainder depends on *physical* path length or stabilisation protocol after known resolution and alignment effects have been accounted for. The resolution of an exported image can be changed without changing the light path — that manipulation is an important negative control.

3. **Independently realised terms programme** — for F = ma, list the force, mass, and acceleration registrations and their calibration histories; compare the constructed ma with the force record using a joint uncertainty model; a repeatable remainder should be fitted as an additional physical component only after the known interactional and instrumental terms have been represented.

4. **Astronomical programme** — begin with spectra for which independent distance information exists; analyse raw spectral registration, wavelength calibration, and line assignment without inserting the distance into the redshift measurement; construct the separate distance trajectory; search for a proposed path functional only after expressing a falsifiable alternative to standard predictions and showing that the result is stable across lines, objects, instruments, and independent distance methods.

### Chapter 11 — Propositions and open obligations (pp. 21–28)

The final chapter consolidates the monograph's representational framework and contains an extended **Afterword: "Representation, Reconstruction, and the Inheritance of Models"** (pp. 23–28).

The opening propositions state: under an expressly chosen resolution Δ and the coverage convention, N_cov(L, Δ) is finite for finite L and grows approximately linearly with extent; a short symbol can denote any of these extents while its surface length remains nearly constant. These statements follow from the chosen representation and need no new claim about the physics of light or gravity. To obtain a theory of measured representations, each application must additionally declare the instrument, the distinction criterion, the usable Greene–Pascal trajectory, uncertainty, correlation, and provenance. To obtain a theory with new physical predictions, it must further exhibit a physical path property, a rule coupling that property to an observable, and data that discriminate the rule from an appropriate conventional model.

The **Afterword** develops FSM's relationship to inherited mathematical notation. Existing models are themselves finite symbolic constructions developed under particular licences, commitments, historical circumstances, instruments, and mathematical practices. Their success lies partly in their capacity to compress long interactional and representational trajectories into compact symbolic forms. Scientific development may be represented as a recursive cycle:

$$\mathcal{I} \longrightarrow \mathcal{R} \longrightarrow \mathcal{S} \longrightarrow \mathcal{C} \longrightarrow \mathcal{M} \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}' \longrightarrow \mathcal{R}'$$

where ℐ is an interaction, ℛ a registration, 𝒮 a symbolic representation, 𝒞 a compression, ℳ a model, 𝓛' a subsequent licence, and ℛ' a reconstruction developed under those commitments. At the level of individual registrations, a structured object is:

$$R_i = (\Delta_i, \rho_i, \omega_i, U_i, H_i, c_i)$$

(finite extent, interactional density, overlap, uncertainty, provenance, cost). The trajectory:

$$\mathcal{T}_P = R_1 \to R_2 \to \cdots \to R_N$$

is prior to any scalar compression. A compression rule under licence 𝓛 maps the trajectory to a scalar descriptor:

$$\mathcal{K}_\mathcal{L} : \mathcal{T}_P \longrightarrow C_P$$

The reconstruction cycle across scientific generations:

$$\mathcal{R}_0 \to \mathcal{C}_1(\mathcal{R}_0) \to \mathcal{R}_1 \to \mathcal{C}_2(\mathcal{R}_1) \to \mathcal{R}_2 \to \cdots$$

An earlier model need not be rejected because a later licence exposes structure it did not retain; reconstruction becomes necessary only where the commitments of the new licence require distinctions the earlier representation removed. The classical integral, finite summations, and the mathematical notation used throughout the monograph are retained as inherited symbolic machinery used provisionally to make the construction possible. The boxed thesis: **"Scientific objects are inherited compressions before they become objects of new construction."**

### Terminological Note (p. 29)

Defines key FSM-specific terms as used in this working monograph:

- **Alphonic scale** — a declared, provisionally distinguishable finite scale; not an SI unit.
- **Greene–Pascal condition** — a criterion for a usable finite symbolic trajectory.
- **Coverage count** — a chosen resolution count.
- **Cost** — requires an indicated component, encoding, and task.
- **Vertical bar** (|) — a declared FSM correspondence between registrations or constructions, not an alternative spelling of an algebraic equals sign.
- **κ** — a comparison remainder whose mathematical type must be supplied by the application.
- **Licence** — the commitments that make a translation or comparison admissible; does not claim that the underlying formal mathematics is invalid.

## Named Propositions and Principles

| Name | Statement |
|------|-----------|
| Coverage linearity | Under expressly chosen resolution Δ, N_cov(L, Δ) is finite for finite L and grows approximately linearly with extent. |
| Surface economy | A short symbol can denote extents of very different N_cov while its surface length remains nearly constant. |
| Cost incomparability | The four costs in 𝒞(L, Δ; P, e) are not ordinarily equal and may not be compared directly across components. |
| Alphonic–GP independence | The Alphonic scale α and the Greene–Pascal condition G_GP are independent: a mark may carry Alphonic-scale candidates without satisfying the GP threshold. |
| Decompression distinction | Printing a compact symbol does not execute the physical operations corresponding to its explicitly resolved representation; decompression is an activity requiring those resources. |
| Geometric vs. interactional extent | A metre gives a nominal distance; an observation gives a distribution of finite responses. The relationship between the two requires an instrument model. |
| Notation licence | Classical mathematical notation (integrals, summations, logarithms) is used as inherited symbolic machinery under a licence; it does not imply those objects are admitted as primitive under FSM. |
| Reconstruction inheritance | Scientific objects are inherited compressions before they become objects of new construction. |
| Cost–consequence bridge | A representational cost becomes physically consequential only when Q_P is independent of the scientist's notation and a coupling to an observable is specified and tested. |
