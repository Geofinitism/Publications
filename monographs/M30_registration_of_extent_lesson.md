# Lesson: Registration of Extent
## M30 — Toward a Finite Symbolic Account of Measurement, Decoherence, and Dynamical Stability

**Author:** Kevin R. Haylett · **Series:** Foundational Measurement Series · **Pages:** 39

This lesson guides the student through the four main movements of *Registration of Extent*: the reversal of the representational order (registration before symbol); the dynamical reconstruction programme (beads, trajectories, attractors, averaging); the application of that programme to quantum measurement language (path integrals, superposition, two-slit); and the account of decoherence and collapse as consequences of system enlargement and representational compression. Exercises move from conceptual analysis to formal construction to provisional experimental design.

---

## Section I: Registration Before Representation (Chs. 1–3, Prefatory Note)

### Overview

The opening movement establishes the methodological reversal that governs the whole monograph. Classical and quantum physics habitually grant the symbolic or mathematical object first — a point, a state, a Hilbert-space vector — and then introduce measurement as the operation by which that object is made observable. FSM reverses this. The physical sequence is: extent → interaction → finite distinction → stabilisation → registration → symbol. Only the last step produces the symbolic object, and the previous steps are its provenance. The Prefatory Note names the central question: what is lost when an extended interactional event is compressed into a point-like symbol?

Chapter 1 states the methodological principle (Registration precedes representation) and its five commitments (extent, interaction, finite distinction, trajectory, stabilisation). Chapter 2 introduces the generonic boundary — the chosen point in a measurement chain at which analogue interaction becomes finite symbol — showing it is operationally real but not necessarily an ontological divide in the world. Chapter 3 replaces the zero-extent point with the finite bead, and isolated beads with the relational bead string.

## Section II: Trajectories, Attractors, and Averaging (Chs. 4–6)

### Overview

Having replaced points with beads and isolated registrations with bead strings, the monograph now develops the dynamical machinery: delay embedding for phase-space reconstruction (Ch. 4), the reformulation of attractors as stable organisations of continuing dynamics rather than stable values (Ch. 5), and the identification of averaging as a potentially destructive form of trajectory compression in nonlinear systems (Ch. 6).

Chapter 4 introduces the delay embedding **X**ₜ = [s(t), s(t−τ), s(t−2τ)] as a finite symbolic relation among measured values — not as coordinates of an independently existing space — and shows that once the string folds through reconstructed phase space, attractor geometry becomes visible without requiring a physically instantiated continuum. Chapter 5 demonstrates with the circular limit cycle that the average of the trajectory coordinates (0, 0) is a point the system never occupies: stable organisation ≠ stable value. Chapter 6 shows that for nonlinear functions F(⟨x⟩) ≠ ⟨F(x)⟩, so averaging before the trajectory has done its work changes or destroys the dynamics.

## Section III: Quantum Language Reconsidered (Chs. 7–12)

### Overview

The dynamical machinery developed in Section II is now applied to the language of quantum mechanics. Each chapter takes a standard quantum-mechanical term or experimental result and subjects it to the FSM question: what measured trajectory preceded this symbol?

Chapter 7 unfolds the path integral as "relational trajectory contributions accumulated before final compression." Chapter 8 identifies the quantum language problem: the standard formalism uses structures permitting indefinitely fine distinction (ℝ, ℂ, ℋ, ψ, ∫ 𝒟[x]) while quantum experiments produce finite registrations (clicks, spots, binary outputs). Chapter 9 reconceives superposition as unresolved dynamical accessibility — the wider system has not yet stabilised into either terminal registration basin — rather than the literal co-existence of symbolic outcomes. Chapter 10 reformulates the two-slit experiment as a single extended interacting system 𝒮 = {source, apertures, materials, surroundings, detector, …} whose boundary conditions change when an aperture is altered. Chapter 11 distinguishes pattern of registrations from registration of a pattern. Chapter 12 treats the click as an end bead relative to a particular analysis, not an absolute physical endpoint.

## Section IV: Decoherence, Collapse, and the Experimental Programme (Chs. 13–19, Appendices A–B)

### Overview

The final movement applies the registration framework to the most contested territory in quantum foundations: decoherence, collapse, and the quantum-to-classical boundary.

Chapter 13 develops the FSM decoherence conjecture: a dynamically stable relational organisation at one interactional scale may become unstable when incorporated into a wider coupled system, whose dynamics then converge toward a coarser stable organisation capable of producing a finite registration. A(S_A) ≠ A(S_{AB}). Chapter 14 treats measurement as physical coarse-graining: the detector is itself a nonlinear dynamical system deliberately engineered to suppress lower-scale detail and produce robust macroscopic registration states. Chapter 15 identifies collapse (|ψ⟩ → |0⟩) as at least partly a representational discontinuity generated when an extended dynamical process is compressed into a finite symbolic state. Chapter 16 proposes one world of extent — replacing the quantum-to-classical boundary with successive dynamical transformations across scale in a single world. Chapter 17 condenses the argument into the six-step FSM registration principle and the full emerging trajectory sequence. Chapters 18–19 present the two methodological routes side by side and state the experimental commitment. Appendices A–B provide the working vocabulary and six provisional statements for later testing.
