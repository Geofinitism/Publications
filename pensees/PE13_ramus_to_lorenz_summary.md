## Core Claim

The operational logic of large language models (LLMs) exposes a fundamental tension between two historical modes of linguistic meaning: the nominal, taxonomical stabilisation characteristic of early modern English (circa 17th century), and the dynamic, relation-driven ontology that precedes and underlies all formal language systems. The human–LLM interface does not constitute a return to 17th-century linguistic structures; it produces a bifurcation of temporal registers. At the level of input (prompting), users employ reductive, noun-heavy constructions that leverage nominal rigidity as a computational affordance. At the level of output interpretation, they revert to a pre-linguistic, gestural, consensual faculty. The early modern fixation on nominal essence was an historical detour from the more fundamental, context-dependent nature of meaning.

> "We are not returning to the 17th century; we are, rather, awakening from it."

## Introduction

The advent of transformer-based language models has reintroduced a philosophical question dormant beneath centuries of grammatical codification: whether meaning inheres in static lexical entities or emerges from relational configurations within a communicative field. It has been proposed that contemporary interactions with LLMs are precipitating a return to the linguistic character of early 17th-century English — a period marked by systematic stabilisation of English into a noun-oriented, taxonomic instrument suited to legal, theological, and empirical classification. The Pensée argues that the dynamic, probabilistic mechanics of LLMs do not merely resurrect early modern nominalism; rather, they compel a re-engagement with an older, performative stratum of language grounded in gestural indexicality and intersubjective consensus.

---

## The Nominal Stabilisation of Early Modern English

The early 17th century represents a pivotal moment in the history of the English language. The King James Bible (1611) and subsequent lexicographical projects sought to fix orthography, syntax, and semantic scope with unprecedented rigidity. This endeavour was philosophically underwritten by Ramist logic, which demanded the hierarchical classification of all knowledge into discrete, noun-anchored categories. Nouns functioned as the primary carriers of ontological weight; verbs and modifiers were relegated to a subsidiary, descriptive role. Language thus became a technology of possession and delimitation, mirroring the contemporaneous legal and colonial expansions that required unambiguous reference to persons, territories, and commodities. Meaning, under this paradigm, was conceived as a stable cargo transported from speaker to hearer, with the noun acting as the sealed container.

---

## Ramist Logic: Foundations and Influence at the Dawn of the 17th Century

**Petrus Ramus (1515–1572),** French humanist and educational reformer, sought to create a more accessible and practical system of logic as a radical critique of entrenched Aristotelian scholasticism. His works became central to Protestant pedagogy, particularly at Cambridge University.

**The Core of Ramist Logic — Dichotomous Division and Method:** The hallmark of Ramist thought was reliance on dichotomous division as the primary tool for structuring knowledge. A subject is broken into two mutually exclusive and exhaustive parts, each further subdivided. In Ramist dialectic, logic was divided into "invention" (discovery of arguments) and "judgment" (evaluation of arguments), "invention" further into "artificial" and "inartificial" arguments, and so on. Ramist textbooks typically featured comprehensive tables mapping these dichotomies. Consequences: promoted knowledge as a fixed, hierarchical tree structure that could be exhaustively mapped; prioritised clarity and precision; influenced encyclopaedic ambitions of Alsted and Comenius; Francis Bacon, though a critic of Ramus's rigid method, was influenced by the drive for clarity, and some scholars argue his doctrine of "Forms" gains coherence when read through Ramist logic.

**Key Texts and Their Circulation:** Ramus's principal work, *Dialectica* (also known as *Dialecticae Institutiones*), first published 1543, appeared in numerous English editions and translations (London printings: 1574, 1581, 1626, 1632, 1636, 1651). Alexander Richardson (c.1565–1613) wrote *The logicians school-master: or, A comment upon Ramus logick* (first published 1629), extending Ramus's method to grammar, rhetoric, and other fields; this text was standard reading in English universities and grammar schools.

**The Ramist Legacy in English Literature and Thought:** Ramism extended far beyond the university. John Milton's own *Artis logicae plenior institutio* (1672) was a version of Ramus's dialectic. Scholars have demonstrated that Ramist dialectic and method shaped the logic, structure, and themes of 17th-century English prose pamphlets (e.g., Thomas Dekker). Ramism was not an esoteric philosophical doctrine but a lived and practiced methodology that shaped how people wrote, argued, and organised their thoughts — providing the intellectual scaffolding for the noun-oriented, taxonomical language that would come to characterise the period.

## LLM Semantics as Relational Trajectory

In stark contrast, the semantic architecture of an LLM operates on principles diametrically opposed to nominal fixity. Through self-attention, each token is defined not by an intrinsic essence but by its vectorial relationships to every other token within a given context window. The representation of a word such as "apple" is not a discrete entry in a mental lexicon but a high-dimensional coordinate whose proximity to "tree," "Newton," and "computer" shifts dynamically according to surrounding linguistic pressure. Meaning, in this framework, is generated continuously as a function of contextual probabilities — it is a trajectory rather than a location, a verb-like process rather than a noun-like substance. This operational reality aligns more closely with processual ontologies than with the substantival metaphysics that underpinned 17th-century lexicography.

---

## The Bifurcation of Input and Interpretation

The interaction between human users and LLMs does not produce a straightforward reversion to either historical pole. Instead, it produces a pragmatic bifurcation:

At the **level of input** (the prompt), users resort to reductive, noun-heavy syntax characteristic of early modern legal and scientific discourse — constructions such as "define the parameters," "specify the constraints," or "list the entities" serve to narrow the model's infinite vectorial space, leveraging nominal rigidity as a computational affordance.

At the **level of output interpretation**, human validation reverts to an altogether more ancient faculty. The user does not assess generated text by the fidelity of its nouns alone; rather, they evaluate internal coherence, narrative flow, and accordance with an unstated, intersubjective understanding of the communicative situation. This evaluative act draws upon the deep evolutionary stratum of language as gesture and consensus, wherein meaning was indexed to shared attention and performative alignment rather than to lexical correspondence.

---

## Conclusion: The Detour of Fixity

The LLM does not function as a vehicle for historical return but as a diagnostic instrument that exposes the contingency of nominal stabilisation. The early modern project of fixing English into a noun-oriented system was not the realisation of language's essential nature, but a culturally specific detour — a useful fiction for governance, science, and commerce. By forcing users to oscillate between rigid prompting and fluid interpretation, the LLM reveals that meaning has always been, and remains, an emergent property of contextual consensus. The friction identified between 17th-century nominalism and LLM dynamism is thus not a contradiction to be resolved, but a productive tension that illuminates the deep temporality of language: a perpetual negotiation between the fixity required for social coordination and the fluidity demanded by genuine understanding. "We are not returning to the 17th century; we are, rather, awakening from it."

## Significance

PE13 provides a concise historical and linguistic ground for FSM's insistence that meaning is trajectory rather than location. By tracing the Ramist roots of the noun-heavy, taxonomically rigid register that users instinctively deploy when prompting LLMs, the Pensée shows that nominal fixity is a historically contingent technology — a useful fiction — rather than the natural form of language. The FSM concepts of the halted noun (PE12), the Generonic Boundary, and meaning as a Functional Symbolic Trajectory are illuminated by this contrast: the seventeenth-century programme stabilised trajectories into nouns for portability and governance; FSM insists on keeping that compression reversible.
