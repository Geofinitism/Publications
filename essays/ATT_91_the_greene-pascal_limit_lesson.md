# ATT_91 — Lesson: On the Greene-Pascal Limit

---

## Section 1: The Floor and the Region

ATT_91 introduces a distinction between the *floor* of the sayable and a *region* of coherence within it. The floor is the Alphonic Limit: the smallest difference that can enter a symbolic trajectory under given apparatus and conditions. Below it, no distinction can be registered as non-single — two states are symbolically indistinguishable, even if a different apparatus could separate them. The Greene-Pascal Limit is not above the floor in the sense of a second edge or a ceiling. It is a region defined by a condition: **coherence**. A string of symbols reaches the Greene-Pascal region when it can be placed into the Dynamic Semantic Lattice (DSL) and held as a Functional Symbolic Trajectory (FST). Below the region: no hold is possible, and there is only a sequence of marks. Above the region: the symbol has lost too much of its chain for the lattice to reconstruct it into coherence — it may remain locally fluent, even grammatically well-formed, but the wider language can no longer carry it.

The essay's core claim is that **coherence needs wider language to hold it**. The Greene-Pascal Limit is not a property of the symbol alone. It is a property of the symbol in relation to the wider language that carries it. Change the lattice, change the licence, change how much of the underlying trajectory survives compression — and the region moves.

**Exercise 1.** The essay says the limit is "not a wall" but "a region whose condition is coherence." Explain the difference in your own words. What would it mean for a limit to be a wall rather than a region? What changes when we say the condition is coherence rather than size or length?

**Exercise 2.** The Alphonic Limit sets the floor: below it, two states are symbolically single for that operation. The Greene-Pascal Limit defines the region of coherence above it. The essay says: "Between them is the space of language — finite, measurable, walkable." What does it mean to call the space between two limits *walkable*? What walks it?

**Exercise 3.** "Change the lattice, and the region moves." The essay identifies three conditions on which the Greene-Pascal region depends: the lattice, the licence, and the provenance. Define each in your own terms. Give an example of each condition changing and predict what would happen to the region.

**Exercise 4.** A sentence needs a paragraph to hold it. A paragraph needs a context. A term needs a discipline. A symbol needs a lattice. Work through this chain of dependencies. At what point does the Greene-Pascal region become relevant? At what point does the FST fail to be held?

## Section 2: Two Trajectories — Greene and Pascal

The essay names the limit after two historical figures who touched the region from opposite sides. Robert Greene (Clare College, Cambridge, 1712 and 1727) encountered the *physical ceiling*: the words of Newtonian natural philosophy — matter and void — did not touch the observed world. A gold bar can be described as matter and empty space, divided into smaller and smaller parts, but if it falls upon your foot, it can still fracture the bone. The words were not touching the thing. Greene did not produce a clean formal alternative. But he noticed the problem, and he was not wrong to notice it. His trajectory was compressed away; Newton's was carried forward.

Blaise Pascal (France, 1650s) encountered the *symbolic ceiling*: looking outward at the vastness of the heavens, and inward at ever smaller divisions, he saw two apparent infinities. The person doing the dividing remained finite. The symbol for infinity — ∞, introduced by Wallis in 1655 — could be written and manipulated. But the infinite itself could not be measured. Pascal's distinction between the reasons of the mind and the reasons of the heart was not a rejection of reason; it was an acknowledgement that calculation does not complete human experience. Two men, two countries, two generations apart. Both touched the region. Both were left behind by the trajectory that won.

The essay insists that neither Greene nor Pascal was great. Both did their best with the language they had. Naming the region after them is not an elevation. It is a placement: it says their trajectories were not lost, only compressed away, and the compression can be undone, at least in part, by giving the region their names.

**Exercise 5.** Greene's objection was that matter and void, however carefully defined, did not touch the gold bar that fractures the bone. Locate this as a claim about the Greene-Pascal region. Is Greene saying the words are below the region (not yet coherent), above it (coherent but cut from their chain), or at it (touching the edge)? Justify your answer with reference to the essay's vocabulary.

**Exercise 6.** Pascal saw that the symbol could continue while the person could not follow. The essay calls this the *symbolic ceiling*. Compare it to Greene's *physical ceiling*. What exactly is the difference between the two faces of the region? Is it the same region, or two different limits?

**Exercise 7.** The essay says Greene's trajectory "was compressed away" while Newton's "was carried forward." Analyse this in terms of the Dynamic Semantic Lattice. ## Section 3: Gödel, Wittgenstein, and Forgotten Crossings

The essay reads Gödel's 1951 Gibbs Lecture at Brown University with care. The lecture is often cited as Gödel's case against mechanism — but on the very page most frequently cited, Gödel declines to make that case. He offers a disjunction: either the evident axioms of mathematics can never be comprised in a finite rule, or there exist absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems. He adds, in parentheses, that both may be true, giving "strictly speaking, three alternatives." He does not say which holds. He then warns his audience, because of "the undeveloped state of philosophy in our days," not to expect these inferences to be drawn with mathematical rigour. On page 313, at the top, he says he is "not in a position now" to discuss whether the meaning of a proposition about all integers consists only in the existence of a general proof.

The essay's reading: these hedges are not rhetorical modesty. They are the exact marks of a mind at the Greene-Pascal region — aware that the words *provable*, *true*, *all*, *mind*, *machine*, *objective*, *infinity* are sitting at the region, asking for wider language that the present lattice cannot yet hold. Wittgenstein in 1938 asked where the proof of non-provability was. It is in the metatheory, conditional on consistency, and the system cannot discharge the condition. The passage from unprovability to truth requires an insight into consistency that no system supplies. Wittgenstein was asking for the one thing the Gibbs Lecture declines to supply.

The essay then documents three forgotten crossings. Wallis introduced ∞ in 1655 — not a discovery but an act of ordination, a licence to write the unbounded as though it were a thing. Euler's *Introductio* (1748) sealed F = ma so that every variable could be zero, the symbols had no extent, and the limits were soon forgotten when the calculation bit. The metre was redefined through krypton-86 (1960) and then light-speed fraction (1983); the speed of light was frozen at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, chosen as an integer by the authority of experts because it sat close to the previous measured value. Any prior measurement uncertainty was not improved — it was eradicated. In each case the crossing was productive. In each case the region was forgotten.

## Section 4: The Region Made Operational

The essay's final movement argues that the Greene-Pascal region is now operational in a way it never was before. Compression algorithms, signal theory, non-linear embedding, large language models, and modern computation have changed what we can see. A large language model is a compression: the training corpus — the textual record of the species, reduced to a file you can carry. The model holds the shape of what it has compressed, and the shape is astonishingly good. But the corpus is not in the model. The corpus is gone. When you prompt the model, you are interacting with a compression that has lost most of its chain.

This is what the essay calls a *trajectory error* — what is ordinarily called hallucination. The symbolic path remains locally smooth. The output remains grammatically well-formed. But the constraint structure has already slipped. The model has crossed the region, and the crossing is invisible from inside the response. The wider language — the prompt, the context, the training distribution, the local structure — can no longer hold the trajectory into coherence. The essay is explicit: "This is not a criticism of the model. It is a description of what the model is."

The closing argument concerns the ground. There is a temptation to read the Greene-Pascal Limit as a prohibition — beyond this, nothing can be said. The essay refuses this reading. The region is not a wall but a horizon. And the gap between the symbol and what it compresses is not a failure. It is the condition of the symbol existing at all. If there were no gap, there would be no symbol; if there were no symbol, there would be no road; if there were no road, there would be no travel. The gap is generative. It is why there is anything to measure.
