## Overview

This lesson works through ATT_88's argument in four stages: (1) the noun as an instrument of symbolic stabilisation — what the inventory does and why it works at middle scales; (2) the historical making of the scientific noun and Pascal's early identification of the gap between words and processes; (3) where thing-language breaks down — the very small, the very large and the highly complex — and the registration sequence that underlies all scientific representation; (4) the admissible noun and the reversibility requirement: what it would mean for scientific language to move in both directions between process and noun.

The essay's central proposition: *A noun is a halted trajectory — a process compressed into symbolic stability.* The lesson develops what this means for how we read scientific language, what it conceals, and why recovering the verb is the next foundational task of science.

## Section 1 — The Noun as Instrument: What the Inventory Does

**Core question:** Why does thing-language work so well, and what does it suppress in working?

**The room as starting point.** The essay opens with an ordinary room: table, chair, window, book, lamp, glass. The language seems natural, as if the world arrived already divided into things. Yet nothing in the room is still in any absolute sense. The table exchanges heat with the air, accumulates marks, alters chemically. The hand that lifts the glass is itself blood circulating, nerves firing, cells being repaired and replaced. The noun *table* works not because the table has physically stopped, but because its relevant organisation persists long enough for the observational and practical purposes of the moment.

**The key formal condition.** Let T_o represent the duration of observation or practical use, and T_c represent the characteristic duration over which the configuration changes enough to disrupt its classification. Where

> T_o ≪ T_c

noun-like treatment is usually effective. The omitted dynamics remain present, but they do not materially disturb the intended action. The distinction between noun and verb is therefore not absolute; it is a distinction of compression, purpose, duration and scale.

**What the inventory suppresses.** An inventory contains no motion. It tells us what has been counted as present, but not how the counted objects arrived, what sustains them, how they are changing or what relations allowed each to be distinguished as one item rather than another. The list removes sequence, most relations, and almost everything that sustains the objects as they are. This is why the inventory works: a complete account of the room would not merely be long — it would be impossible to complete, because the act of describing would itself alter the room and extend the history that required description.

**The key formulation.** *A noun retains a distinction while releasing us from the requirement to repeat the complete trajectory of what has been distinguished.* The table can be named, remembered, sold, repaired and discussed by people who have never occupied the same room. The word travels where the table does not. The stopping is not physical. It is symbolic.

**Exercises:**
1. Apply the T_o ≪ T_c condition to three different objects or phenomena: a stone, a flame, and a language. For each, identify T_o (under what conditions of use?) and T_c (how quickly does the configuration change?). Does the noun work equally well in all three cases? What changes at the boundary?
2. The essay says "the stopping is not physical. It is symbolic." Explain in your own words what this distinction means. What would it mean for stopping to be physical? What does it mean for it to be symbolic?
3. List five things that an inventory of a room does NOT record. For each, explain under what scientific question the omitted information might become consequential.
4. "A thing, in this practical sense, is not the opposite of a process. A thing is a process whose transformation is sufficiently slow, recurrent or constrained relative to the observational window." Take one scientific "thing" (a species, an atom, a memory, a gene) and describe the process it compresses. What is the observational window within which it remains stable?

## Section 2 — The Historical Making of the Scientific Noun and Pascal's Warning

**Core question:** How did processes become things in early modern science, and what gap did Pascal identify in that project?

**Making distinctions travel.** In the seventeenth century, human societies had named and classified things for millennia. What changed in early modern Europe was the scale and institutional organisation of the work. The Baconian natural history programme encouraged accumulation of particulars before the premature construction of universal systems. The Royal Society developed practices of witnessing, reporting, repetition and publication. Hooke's *Micrographia* organised instrumentally mediated distinctions into images and names that could be encountered by readers who had never looked through Hooke's instrument. Boyle's experimental histories retained a disciplined symbolic trajectory judged sufficient for another investigator to understand, assess or repeat the work.

The crucial move: *the thing became portable because selected distinctions were detached from the complete trajectory of the encounter.* A plant could be removed from its environment, pressed, labelled and placed within a collection. A phenomenon that occurred once in one room might be re-registered by readers elsewhere. *The world did not literally enter the book. The book carried finite marks from which a reader could reconstruct enough of the original distinctions to participate in the claim.*

**The scientific noun as technology.** To call the noun a scientific technology is not to suggest it was consciously designed. Its technological character lies in what it permits: a living plant becomes a specimen; the specimen becomes a description; the description becomes a class; the class receives a name; the name circulates after the specimen has decayed. A moving body becomes a trajectory; the trajectory becomes a function; the function can be analysed in the absence of the body. At each stage, the language gains stability and loses trajectory. *The compression worked so well that the existence of the compression could be forgotten.*

**Pascal's unease.** Pascal stood within this transformation rather than outside it. He was a mathematician, experimenter, designer of calculating machinery and participant in seventeenth-century debates concerning pressure, vacuum, proof and method. Yet his writings preserve a persistent unease about the relation between words, definitions and what they attempt to hold.

His clearest formulation: "One cannot define all things, nor demonstrate all propositions... There must therefore be words that are understood without definition." The essay reads this not as a concession to linguistic imperfection, but as identification of the *operational terminus* of a particular community's practice. The words understood without definition are understood because they have reached the limit of that practice — not because they have somehow reached the thing itself.

*Pascal's concern can be read as a warning from within the emerging scientific language: words are necessary instruments, but they cannot become the very things they are used to distinguish.* The definition is a tool, not a revelation. It coordinates action without containing the complete trajectory from which it was formed.

**Nouns that move.** It would be a mistake to treat nouns as fixed while verbs alone carry change. Words move historically. The word *atom* carries a historical implication of indivisibility, yet the scientific structures admitted under the word have changed profoundly: philosophical minima, chemical units, internally structured systems, probabilistic descriptions, instrumentally reconstructed patterns. The same applies to *element*, *species*, *force*, *field*, *gene*, *information*, *intelligence*, *memory*, *particle* and *state*. The noun survives while the scientific object changes beneath it. This is close to Kuhn's historical insight: the continuity of language can conceal discontinuity in the scientific world.

## Section 3 — Where Thing-Language Breaks Down: Registration Before Representation

**Core question:** Where do the limits of noun-language become most visible, and what is the sequence through which all scientific representation passes?

**Three boundaries.** The limits of thing-language become most visible at three scales: the very small, the very large and the highly complex.

At very small scales, the scientific entity may be inferred from interactions, traces, deflections, pulses and distributions. The object is not encountered in the same manner as a table. It is reconstructed from registrations produced through instruments and interpreted through mathematics. *Object-language is most secure when different instruments, procedures and representations converge upon a stable distinction.*

At very large scales, the object may exceed any single observational frame. A galaxy is reconstructed from radiation arriving at different times, through different instruments and under changing models of distance, motion and structure. A climate system is assembled from distributed measurements, historical records, proxies, models and classifications. *The noun compresses this distributed work into a single apparent presence.* The unity may be scientifically productive, but it is a symbolic achievement rather than a single encounter.

In highly complex systems, the relevant behaviour arises through changing relations, feedback, memory and history. An ecosystem is not explained by listing organisms; a language is not explained by listing words; a mind is not explained by listing neurons; a neural network is not explained by enumerating parameters. The components matter, but the behaviour under investigation occurs through changing relations. *At each boundary, process returns.*

**The circular route.** When noun-language is extended into these domains, the scientific language performs a familiar move: a recurring pattern is registered, the pattern is given a label, the label becomes a state, property, component or object, and the noun then enters explanation as though it had existed independently before the measurements that produced it:

> interaction → distinction → registration → pattern → classification → [entity] → property → observed effect

This may be a legitimate explanatory compression, but the circular route must remain recoverable when the distinction is uncertain or scale-dependent.

**Registration before representation.** Before something can be represented, a distinction must be registered. This elementary sequence is often skipped because scientific language begins with already-formed symbols:

> interaction → distinction → registration → symbolic representation

Registration is not passive copying. An instrument, observer or material system responds selectively. Some differences are stabilised; others are lost. A photographic surface, detector, ruler, balance, sensor, nervous system or written language does not retain the complete event. Each has finite resolution, range, geometry, history and cost. *The registered mark is therefore not the event itself. It is a finite consequence of an interaction.*

The complete trajectory of scientific knowledge:

> worldly interaction → registration → symbol → circulation → re-registration

The scientific object is what remains after an interaction has been registered, symbolised and stabilised sufficiently to re-enter language. It is neither a mere invention nor an unmediated thing delivered intact from the world. It is a stabilised symbolic construction constrained by repeated interactions and shared practices.

**Mathematics and the world made still.** Mathematics is often presented as the language best suited to change — it describes velocity, acceleration, flow, oscillation, growth, decay. Yet to calculate change, mathematics must first render change into stable symbolic constructions. A movement becomes a trajectory; a trajectory becomes a function; a changing relation becomes an equation. *The danger begins when the mathematical object is returned to the world without restoring what was removed during its construction.* The curve is spoken of as though it were the trajectory itself. The state vector becomes the physical state. *A compressed symbolic trajectory should not be returned to the world as though nothing had been removed.*

**The image inside the compression.** JPEG compression provides a model for understanding what is retained through transformation. An image is divided, transformed and represented through coefficients. The visible image is no longer present in the encoded structure in the form in which an observer sees it. Yet enough organised distinction remains for an image to be reconstructed under an agreed decoding process. *The image is not sitting inside the file as a tiny picture. It is recoverable through a trajectory of transformation.* The same applies to meaning in language embeddings: meaning may not be located inside an isolated vector any more than an image is visibly located inside one JPEG coefficient.

## Section 4 — The Admissible Noun and Recovering the Verb

**Core question:** What would it mean for scientific language to move in both directions between process and noun?

**The admissible noun.** An admissible noun preserves enough of its originating trajectory for the intended scientific purpose. Admissibility is contextual, historical and scale-dependent. The noun *water* is highly admissible for drinking, transport, plumbing and much of chemistry. The phrase *two liquids* may be admissible within a carefully specified thermodynamic discussion, yet misleading when it suggests two enduring substances occupying an ordinary glass. The noun *point* is admissible within a formal geometry, but not as a direct description of an ink mark whose extent is being measured. The noun *memory* is admissible as a broad functional term, but misleading when it encourages the belief that an unchanged object is retrieved from storage.

Instead of asking only what the thing is, we ask: What distinctions produced the noun? How were those distinctions registered? Which instruments and symbolic procedures were involved? Over what duration does the classification remain stable? At what scale does it apply? Which transformations cause the classification to fail? What uncertainty accompanies it? Which parts of the originating process were omitted? *Admissibility is therefore not a binary property. It is a profile of conditions under which the noun remains trustworthy.*

**Recovering the verb.** Science need not abandon its nouns. The task is not to eliminate compression, but to make it reversible when the omitted trajectory becomes consequential. Scientific language should be able to move in both directions:

> process → noun (for communication, classification and calculation)  
> noun → reconstructed process (for explanation, criticism and boundary testing)

The problem is not nounification alone. It is *irreversible nounification* — compression that cannot be undone when the conditions of the original measurement become relevant again.

A mature scientific language would permit its stable things to be unfolded back into the registrations, relations, transformations and uncertainties from which they were formed. Provenance would not be an optional historical note; it would be part of the meaning of the scientific object. Classification would remain indispensable, but categories would retain the trajectories by which their boundaries were established. Instruments would remain extensions of observation, but their selective registrations would not be mistaken for unmediated access to the world.

**The shortest form.** The essay's conclusion in its briefest expression:

> *Science progressed by learning to turn verbs into nouns. Its next foundational task may be learning when to turn the nouns back into verbs.*

**Returning to the room.** The room is full of both things and processes — not because reality has been divided into two kinds of being, but because language can halt change at different levels of resolution. The table remains a table. Nothing in the argument requires these distinctions to be abandoned. What has changed is the reader's awareness of what the nouns accomplish: they are temporary stabilisations within continuing trajectories, and their stability is an operational achievement whose conditions can be specified, tested and revised.
