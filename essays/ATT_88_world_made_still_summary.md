## Core Claim

A noun is a halted trajectory. More fully: a noun is a process compressed into symbolic stability. Science acquired extraordinary power by performing this compression systematically — dividing, naming, cataloguing, measuring and representing a changing world as a collection of stable things. But the success of the noun also concealed the compression by which it was made. The next foundational task of science is not to abandon nouns, but to recover the verbs from which its nouns were formed: to make compression reversible when the omitted trajectory becomes consequential.

---

## Overview

*The World Made Still* traces the historical and conceptual trajectory by which processes became scientific things. Starting from an ordinary room and an inventory of its objects, the essay asks why noun-language works so well in middle-scale everyday life, how it became the dominant vehicle of scientific knowledge in the seventeenth century, where its limits become visible at the very small, the very large and in complex systems, and what a more disciplined account of scientific language would require.

The essay develops the central formulation through progressive stages: the noun as practical compression; the inventory as a purpose-built suppression of motion; the scientific noun as a technology of portability; Pascal's early warning about the gap between words and processes; the historical movement of scientific terms across paradigms; the scale-dependent admissibility of noun-treatment; registration as the step before representation; mathematics as a second-order compression that must be returned to measurement conditions; the Functional Symbolic Trajectory account of meaning; and the admissible noun as one that preserves enough of its originating trajectory for the intended scientific purpose.

The essay is explicitly marked as a first draft requiring future deepening through primary sources in the history of science.

## Structure and Key Argument

**Entry: A Note Before the Room.** The essay opens by positioning the reader as already measuring: measuring the light on the page, the shape of letters, the rhythm of sentences, testing these against a history of prior reading. This is not a metaphor — it is a description of what reading is. Every act of understanding is an act of comparison. The essay's central object — the noun — is introduced as an instrument, not a container.

**A Room Full of Things.** A room containing a table, chair, window, book, lamp and glass. The language seems so natural that its operation disappears from view. Yet nothing in the room is still in any absolute sense. The table exchanges heat with the air, accumulates marks, alters chemically. The success of the noun rests precisely on this omission. *A noun retains a distinction while releasing us from the requirement to repeat the complete trajectory of what has been distinguished.*

**An Inventory Contains No Motion.** An inventory is not a complete account of the room. It is a purpose-built compression. The list removes sequence, most relations, and almost everything that sustains the objects as they are. *The stopping is not physical. It is symbolic.* The key formal relation: let T_o represent the duration of observation or practical use, and T_c the characteristic duration over which the configuration changes enough to disrupt its classification. Where

> T_o ≪ T_c,

noun-like treatment is usually effective. The process changes slowly relative to the observer's window; the omitted dynamics do not materially disturb the intended action.

**The World Made Portable.** The seventeenth century enlarged the machinery of symbolic stabilisation. Observation, collection, measurement, correspondence, demonstration and publication became increasingly coordinated. Distinctions were not merely made; they were prepared for travel. The Baconian programme, the Royal Society's practices of witnessing and replication, Hooke's *Micrographia*, Boyle's experimental histories, the *Philosophical Transactions* — these created a continuing route by which observations could enter institutional memory. The noun became one of the principal vehicles of that travel: a named specimen could recur in different reports; a property could be assigned to an object; a class could contain many instances.

**The Scientific Noun.** The scientific noun is not a label placed over a pre-existing stable object. It is a technology. Its technological character lies in what it permits: it compresses a potentially unbounded process into a repeatable symbolic unit that can be entered into an inventory, placed within a hierarchy, joined to a measurement, connected to a property, represented by a variable and carried into calculation. At each stage, *the language gains stability and loses trajectory.* The problem is not that omission occurs. The problem arises when the omitted trajectory becomes necessary to the claim while the compression continues to be treated as complete.

**Pascal's Unease.** Blaise Pascal — mathematician, experimenter, designer of calculating machinery — identified a persistent unease about the relation between words, definitions and what they attempt to hold. His clearest formulation appears in *De l'esprit géométrique*: "One cannot define all things, nor demonstrate all propositions... There must therefore be words that are understood without definition." These words are not understood because they have reached the thing itself. They are understood because they have reached the *operational terminus* of a particular community's practice. Pascal's warning: words are necessary instruments, but they cannot become the very things they are used to distinguish. The essay reads this as the condition of symbolic communication — not a failure, but a gap that must remain visible and recoverable.

**Nouns That Move.** A scientific noun is not stable merely because its grammatical form presents something as a thing. Words move historically. The word *atom* carries a historical implication of indivisibility, yet the scientific structures admitted under the word have changed profoundly. The same applies to *element*, *species*, *force*, *field*, *gene*, *information*, *intelligence*, *memory*, *particle* and *state*. The noun survives while the scientific object changes beneath it. This is close to the historical insight associated with Thomas Kuhn: scientific change does not always consist of adding new facts to terms whose meanings remain constant. The continuity of language can conceal discontinuity in the scientific world.

**Why Thing-Language Works.** The historical success of noun-language requires explanation. Thing-language works because much of human activity occurs within a middle range of scales and durations. At this scale, persistence dominates transformation within the observational window. The essay proposes a scale-dependent account of scientific language: *Noun-language is most reliable where persistence dominates transformation within the observational window. Process-language becomes increasingly necessary where transformation, relation and registration dominate apparent persistence.*

**Where Things Begin to Move Again.** The limits of thing-language become most visible at three boundaries: the very small, the very large and the highly complex. At very small scales, the scientific entity is inferred from interactions, traces, deflections and distributions — never encountered as a table is encountered. At very large scales, the object exceeds any single observational frame and is assembled symbolically from spatially and temporally distributed measurements. In highly complex systems, the relevant behaviour arises through changing relations, feedback, memory and history. In each case, process returns. The scientific language then performs a familiar move:

> interaction → distinction → registration → pattern → classification → [entity] → property → observed effect

The entity appears as the cause of the registrations from which it was inferred. This circular route must remain recoverable when the distinction is uncertain or scale-dependent.

**Registration Before Representation.** Before something can be represented, a distinction must be registered. The elementary sequence often skipped in scientific language:

> interaction → distinction → registration → symbolic representation

Registration is not passive copying. An instrument, observer or material system responds selectively; some differences are stabilised, others lost. The complete trajectory:

> worldly interaction → registration → symbol → circulation → re-registration

The scientific object is neither a mere invention nor an unmediated thing delivered intact from the world. It is a stabilised symbolic construction constrained by repeated interactions and shared practices.

**The Small Scale: The Thing After the Event.** At the smallest scales, the noun often arrives after the event. An instrument registers a pulse, trace, distribution or deflection; this is placed within an experimental arrangement, corrected, compared with background, transformed mathematically, assessed against an inherited theoretical structure; repeated events are gathered into a class; the class receives a name; ordinary language then states that the named entity produced the trace. This reversal is scientifically useful, but the language can obscure how dependent the entity is upon the total measurement system.

**The Large Scale: The Object Assembled Across Time.** At the largest scales, the object is too extended to be observed as one. A galaxy is reconstructed from radiation arriving at different times through different instruments; a climate system is assembled from distributed measurements, historical records, proxies, models and classifications. The noun compresses this distributed work into a single apparent presence. The process must remain visible in the form of provenance, uncertainty, model dependence and admissible alternatives.

**Mathematics and the World Made Still.** Mathematics is often presented as the language best suited to change. Yet to calculate change, mathematics must first render change into stable symbolic constructions. A movement becomes a trajectory, a trajectory a function, a changing relation an equation, a local comparison a derivative. The danger: when the mathematical object is returned to the world without restoring what was removed during its construction. The curve is spoken of as though it were the trajectory itself. The probability distribution becomes a property possessed by an object. *A compressed symbolic trajectory should not be returned to the world as though nothing had been removed.*

**When the Inventory Fails.** Complex systems expose the limits of explanation by inventory. An ecosystem is not explained by listing organisms; a language is not explained by listing words; a mind is not explained by listing neurons. The components matter, but the behaviour under investigation occurs through changing relations. Memory offers a clear example: ordinary language treats memory as a stored thing we have or retrieve, yet remembering is an activity in the present — traces, habits and dispositions influence a new reconstruction. *The failure of the inventory is also a failure of isolated symbol-language. In complex systems, the relation may carry more explanatory force than the listed components.*

**Meaning Is Not Contained in the Noun.** The noun–verb distinction is the visible entrance to a deeper problem. The central opposition is not between two grammatical classes but between isolated symbolic objects and functional symbolic trajectories. A noun does not carry one fixed meaning through every context. Meaning is constrained by what precedes the word, what follows it, who uses it, under what circumstances, against which alternatives and with what expected consequences. A reader re-registers a mark through a history of earlier registrations. *The word is not an empty shell, but neither is it a complete container. Meaning is carried through trajectories of relation, expectation and use.*

**The Image Inside the Compression.** The route into this problem began through practical experiments concerning images, compression and language models. JPEG compression divides an image, transforms it and represents it through coefficients; the visible image is no longer present in the encoded structure in the form in which an observer sees it, yet enough organised distinction remains for an image to be reconstructed. A more precise description: the encoded structure retains relations from which a sufficiently similar visual registration can be produced under an agreed decoding process. The image is not sitting inside the file as a tiny picture; it is recoverable through a trajectory of transformation. The same applies to meaning in language embeddings: meaning may not be located inside an isolated vector any more than an image is visibly located inside one JPEG coefficient.

**Trajectory and Reconstruction.** Takens-style phase-space reconstruction offers a useful image for the alternative. A measured sequence from a dynamical system may permit reconstruction of aspects of the wider system through delayed relations. The significance does not lie in any isolated observation considered alone. It lies in the geometry formed across observations. *Meaning is then less like an object retrieved from storage and more like a path reconstructed through a changing symbolic landscape.* This is the basis of the Functional Symbolic Trajectory: a symbolic sequence carries representation, constraint, uncertainty and provenance through time.

**Two Liquids in One Glass.** A contemporary example: a recent popular account of water describes the liquid in an ordinary glass as two different liquids at once. The underlying scientific work concerns recurrent molecular organisations, differences in density and order, possible liquid–liquid transitions and computational classifications. The trajectory of language:

> measured variation → configuration → cluster → structure → state → [two liquids]

Each step is understandable within an appropriate technical context. Taken together, they change the kind of claim being made. A more disciplined statement would say that liquid water exhibits recurrent and distinguishable regimes of molecular organisation whose relative prevalence and stability depend upon temperature, pressure and the method of registration.

**A Noun as a Halted Trajectory.** The central formulation: *A noun is a halted trajectory.* The process has not physically stopped; language has selected a distinction and treated it as sufficiently stable for a purpose. A noun is a process compressed into symbolic stability. Each term matters: *process* indicates interaction, transformation and history; *compression* indicates selective omission; *symbolic* indicates that the retained distinction has entered a system of finite marks and relations; *stability* indicates that the construction can be re-registered with enough consistency to support communication, calculation or action. The noun is therefore an operational achievement rather than a metaphysical guarantee.

**The Admissible Noun.** An admissible noun preserves enough of its originating trajectory for the intended scientific purpose. This requires a change in questioning: instead of asking only what the thing is, we ask — What distinctions produced the noun? How were those distinctions registered? Which instruments and symbolic procedures were involved? Over what duration does the classification remain stable? At what scale does it apply? Which transformations cause the classification to fail? What uncertainty accompanies it? Which parts of the originating process were omitted? *Admissibility is therefore contextual, historical and scale-dependent.*

**Recovering the Verb.** Science need not abandon its nouns. The task is not to eliminate compression, but to make it reversible when the omitted trajectory becomes consequential. Scientific language should be able to move in both directions:

> process → noun (for communication, classification and calculation)  
> noun → reconstructed process (for explanation, criticism and boundary testing)

The problem is not nounification alone. It is irreversible nounification. *The seventeenth-century project made the world portable by stabilising processes into things. The contemporary task is to preserve that achievement while recovering the motion hidden by its success.*

**Returning to the Room.** The room does not arrive as a completed inventory. Distinctions are made within an ongoing interaction. The noun is not merely a label attached to a finished thing. It is a temporary stabilisation within a continuing trajectory. The table can be treated as a thing because its relevant organisation persists within the observational window. At another scale or under another question, it becomes wood fibres, molecular exchanges, stress distributions, industrial history, economic relation or ecological consequence. *Science progressed by learning to turn verbs into nouns. Its next foundational task may be learning when to turn the nouns back into verbs.*
