# ATT_90 — Lesson: The Name Is Not the Mathematics

**Essay:** ATT_90_on_mathematical_linguistics_summary.md  
**Author:** Kevin R. Haylett

## Overview

This lesson works through ATT_90's argument in four stages: (1) the name as compressed pointer — what mathematical naming does and what it conceals; (2) the compression trajectory from person to property, and the test of removing the name to find what remains; (3) mathematics and its surrounding language — the boundary between linguistic and mathematical symbols, the appearance of mathematical things, and abstraction reframed as compression; (4) towards Finite Symbolic Mechanics — the FSM reversal of the naming trajectory, functional symbolic trajectories, and the measurement the essay makes.

The essay's central proposition: *The name is not the mathematics. It is an admissible label, but it is not an admissible explanation. The next step is not to discard the name but to unfold it.*

## Section 1 — The Name as Compressed Pointer

**Core question:** What does a mathematical name actually carry, and what does it conceal?

**The opening observation.** Mathematics presents itself as a language of unusual precision, yet its vocabulary is filled with inherited names: Abelian, Euclidean, Cartesian, Gaussian, Hermitian, Noetherian, Hamiltonian, Lagrangian, Bayesian, Markovian. To the initiated mathematician these terms are efficient — a considerable amount of information appears to arrive with eight letters. But the letters themselves contain almost none of that information. There is nothing about the word *Abelian* that tells a reader that two operations may be performed in either order while preserving the specified relation. The word does not describe the operation, identify the elements, state the rule, or explain what is being compared. It points to Abel — more precisely, to a historical and linguistic community within which the name *Abel* has acquired a particular mathematical trajectory.

**Two readers, same marks.** For a trained mathematician, the word *Abelian* can activate something resembling the full trajectory: Abel → group theory → commutativity → a ○ b = b ○ a. For another reader, the same word may activate almost nothing. This makes *Abelian* interesting not because it is a poor mathematical term, but because it exposes something that normally remains hidden: how much mathematical language depends upon previously stabilised linguistic trajectories. The difference does not lie in the marks on the page. The marks are identical. The difference lies in the trajectory available to the reader.

**The mathematical club.** Specialised communities inevitably develop specialised languages. Once a community has learned the required language, enormously complicated relations can be communicated economically. The price of this economy is less often noticed: terms begin to function as code words. The expert sees "Abelian group" and reconstructs an extensive body of assumptions, definitions, and allowable operations. The novice sees an unfamiliar adjective attached to an unfamiliar noun. Mathematical meaning does not reside entirely in the printed word *Abelian* — somebody unfamiliar with the term cannot extract the mathematical structure merely by inspecting its letters. Nor does it reside entirely in the accompanying symbols = + ∈ ∀ ○, because symbols themselves require learned conventions. Meaning therefore depends upon something larger than the isolated symbol — upon the relationships through which the symbol has been learned and used.

## Section 2 — From Person to Property: The Compression Trajectory

**Core question:** How does a procedure become an apparent property of a mathematical object, and what does the test of removing the name reveal?

**The eponymous trajectory.** Eponymous mathematics makes the compression trajectory particularly easy to see because provenance remains partially visible — we can still see the person inside the adjective. The broad movement runs: Abel → Abelian (a person's name becomes an adjective) → Abelian group (the adjective attaches to a mathematical noun) → *G* is Abelian (the adjective begins to behave as the name of an intrinsic property of G). The same broad movement recurs: Euclid → Euclidean, Gauss → Gaussian, Hilbert → Hilbert space, Hamilton → Hamiltonian, Lagrange → Lagrangian, Noether → Noetherian. The general schematic: procedure → repeatable relation → name → property → apparent object. The trajectory by which the term acquired its meaning gradually disappears, leaving behind what appears to be a stable mathematical object or property.

**Unpacking the compression.** The word *Abelian* can be treated as a compressed representation. Unpacking it produces something much less mysterious: we require some collection of admissible symbols or elements; we require a specified operation; we perform the operation on admissible pairs; we reverse the order of the operands; and we compare the registered results. Schematically: G → {a, b, ...} → ○ → a ○ b → b ○ a → comparison. The mathematical content lies in this network of operations and relations. *Abelian* merely provides a convenient address. There is no practical reason to abolish the word *Abelian*. Once its trajectory is shared it is an extremely efficient linguistic device. The difficulty begins only when the compression is mistaken for the thing being compressed.

**The name-removal test.** A useful diagnostic: remove the name. What remains? If we remove *Abelian*, the operational relation remains available — we can specify what must be done and what relation must hold when the order is changed. The name is dispensable. The operational trajectory is not. Mathematical education usually proceeds in the opposite direction: a term is introduced, given a definition, and subsequently used as though the term carries the definition within itself. Initially the movement is Abelian → definition. After repeated use it becomes definition → Abelian. The compressed label becomes sufficient for an expert reader. This is extraordinarily powerful — one of the reasons mathematics can construct long symbolic arguments without repeatedly expanding every earlier definition. But the same efficiency can conceal the structure through which meaning is being carried.

**Non-eponymous terms: the buried case.** The problem extends beyond eponyms. Words such as *point*, *line*, *set*, *space*, *field*, *function*, *dimension* no longer appear as eponyms. Their histories are buried much more deeply. A point seems to be a thing. A set seems to be a thing. A function seems to be a thing. Yet each is introduced through linguistic and symbolic operations. A definition is established, permitted manipulations are specified, examples are given, relations are repeated, a community stabilises usage. Eventually the term begins to appear self-evident. The word ceases to look like a linguistic construction and starts to look like the name of something that was waiting to be discovered. This transformation — from constructed label to apparent discovery — may be one of the most important effects of mathematical language.

## Section 3 — Mathematics and Its Surrounding Language

**Core question:** Is mathematics embedded in language, or is it a separate structure that language merely describes?

**The boundary question.** Arthur F. Bentley's *Linguistic Analysis of Mathematics* (1932) explicitly turned attention towards the linguistic conditions surrounding mathematical symbolism — his concern was not merely with isolated notation but with the language in which symbols are introduced, interpreted, related, and communicated. At first sight it is tempting to divide mathematical discourse into two regions: ordinary language | mathematical notation. On one side stand words such as *group*, *point*, *space*, *therefore*, *every*, *exists*, and *Abelian*. On the other stand x, +, =, ∀, ∃, ∈. But the boundary is much less obvious than it appears. Mathematical symbols themselves must be learned. Their permissible operations must be taught. Their meanings change historically. Their interpretation depends upon surrounding symbols. The sign = cannot function merely by being present on a page — a reader must possess a trajectory of use that determines what sort of relation is being asserted. The apparent distinction between linguistic and mathematical symbols may conceal a deeper continuity.

**Two pictures of the relationship.** The conventional picture: mathematical structure → symbolic notation → linguistic explanation. Under this view, the mathematical structure exists independently and language is merely employed to communicate it. An alternative picture: symbolic distinction → repeatable operation → stable convention → mathematics. Under this second interpretation, mathematics does not escape the problems of language merely by adopting specialist notation. Its language is more tightly constrained, but it remains language. The symbols are not free of history. They are not free of agreed rules. They are not free of interpretation. And they are certainly not free of compression.

**Abstraction as compression.** What is ordinarily called mathematical abstraction may instead be understood more cautiously as symbolic compression. Suppose several different operational systems exhibit a sufficiently similar registered relation: A₁, A₂, A₃, ..., Aₙ. Rather than continually restating the operational similarity, we assign a symbolic label: A₁, ..., Aₙ → L. The new symbol L allows the previously identified relation to be carried economically into subsequent reasoning. Nothing mysterious need have been extracted from the systems. Nothing need have entered an abstract realm. A finite symbolic community has recognised a repeatable relation and compressed it into another symbol. The new symbol then acquires its own trajectory. Schematically: registered relations → classification → symbolic compression → new symbol. What is ordinarily called abstraction may therefore contain a substantial and sometimes overlooked linguistic component.

**The reader reconstructs the mathematics.** A mathematical expression does not deliver an identical meaning to every observer. The reader reconstructs its meaning using previously learned symbolic relations. What differs between expert and novice is not the physical inscription but the available history of interaction with that inscription and the surrounding symbolic system. Meaning therefore appears less like a substance contained inside a symbol and more like a trajectory capable of being reactivated. The experienced mathematician does not inspect each symbol anew — a previously established path is followed. The trajectory has become sufficiently stable that the intermediate stages largely disappear from conscious view. This may explain something of the peculiar density of advanced mathematics: a short expression can activate an enormous network of prior relations, but only for somebody whose previous symbolic history permits the reconstruction. The page is therefore not carrying all of the mathematics. Part of the mathematics is being supplied by the reader.

## Section 4 — Towards Finite Symbolic Mechanics

**Core question:** What does the FSM framework add to the analysis of mathematical naming, and what does the essay's "measurement" reveal?

**What is actually present.** What is actually present when mathematics is performed, written, communicated, or measured? There are finite marks. There are distinctions between marks. There are rules governing how marks may be combined. There are histories through which those rules have been established. There are communities that recognise particular operations as admissible. There are readers who reconstruct trajectories from finite inscriptions. From an FSM perspective, the name itself cannot provide foundational admissibility. The relevant question becomes: what finite symbolic trajectory allows the named relation to be produced, distinguished, registered, and subsequently recognised? *Abelian*, considered alone, cannot answer this. It identifies neither the registration nor the operation. It is instead a compressed pointer towards a trajectory already stabilised elsewhere. This does not make it useless. It locates its usefulness.

**The FSM reversal.** Traditional mathematical discourse often proceeds by naming and then manipulating named objects: name → property → mathematical object. FSM suggests that it may sometimes be valuable to run the trajectory backwards: symbolic object → permitted operations → registered relations → name. The second form preserves the provenance of the name. The noun remains attached to the process that produced it. The label remains visibly a label. This reversal becomes particularly important when mathematics is applied to measurement — when finite distinctions must cross between physical instrumentation and symbolic representation. At that point, the question of admissibility cannot be answered solely by appealing to a previously agreed mathematical noun. The route by which the symbolic state was produced matters. Its provenance matters. Its uncertainty matters. The distinction by which one state was separated from another matters.

**Functional Symbolic Trajectories.** The term *Abelian* can be considered not as a container holding a mathematical meaning but as a node capable of reactivating a learned trajectory. Very approximately: "Abelian" → prior language → definitions → operations → relations → ... The apparent stability of the word arises from the repeatability of this reconstruction within a sufficiently trained community. The symbol becomes part of a path. The path has history. The path can be compressed. The path can bifurcate. The path can fail to reconstruct in another reader. And the same visible symbol may therefore participate in very different functional trajectories.

**Three trajectories towards future development.** The essay identifies three trajectories that remain open for subsequent work. The first leads towards Finite Symbolic Mechanics and the requirement that symbolic claims retain their admissible operations, provenance, and conditions of registration. The second leads towards Functional Symbolic Trajectories, in which mathematical terms can be studied as nodes within reconstructable histories of symbolic use rather than as isolated containers of meaning. The third leads towards Finite Symbolic Logic, where terms such as identity, equality, implication, and admissibility can be reconsidered not merely as inherited logical primitives but as finite symbolic operations. These developments should follow the historical investigation of Bentley rather than precede it.

**The measurement the essay makes.** The essay has attempted a measurement rather than a theorem. The quantity under investigation was the interval between a name and the finite procedure that the name is later taken to contain. The instrument was comparison: one side of the comparison is the compressed term *Abelian*; the other is the operational trajectory that remains when the term is removed. The result is modest but precise. Three things that ordinary mathematical speech typically presents as one have been separated: (1) the historical name — recoverable as provenance; (2) the operational relation — recoverable as a repeatable comparison; (3) the apparent object-property — recoverable as the success of compression. What remains unmeasured is equally important: the full provenance of terms such as *number*, *set*, or *equality*; a bound on the uncertainty of expert reconstruction; the finite trajectory by which each inherited noun became available. Those are later measurements requiring other instruments: historical reading in the case of Bentley, and an explicit account of admissible operations, registration, and uncertainty in the case of Finite Symbolic Mechanics.
