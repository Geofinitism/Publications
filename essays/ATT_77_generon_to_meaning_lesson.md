## Three Key Ideas

### 1. The Generon as Boundary Mechanism

The **Generon** was introduced to fill a conceptual gap: classical mathematics assumed the existence of numbers rather than explaining how they arise. The Generon is the finite boundary mechanism by which the **Geofinite Continuum** — the non-symbolic condition of possibility — yields admissible symbolic compressions. It operates at the interface between the non-symbolic and the symbolic, encoding finite distinctions produced through interaction. Symbols are therefore not representations of an underlying reality in any direct sense; they are **finite artefacts of boundary processes**, constrained by measurement and resolution.

### 2. Meaning as Trajectory, Not Symbol

A single symbol in isolation carries no determinate meaning. Meaning arises through the unfolding of a **trajectory** in semantic space: each successive symbol constrains the space of possible interpretations, guiding the listener along a path shaped by prior compressions and shared conventions. The receiver does not decode a fixed message — they **reconstruct** an approximate meaning using their own history, context, and prior compressions. Communication succeeds when sender and receiver trajectories converge sufficiently within overlapping basins; perfect alignment is neither expected nor required.

### 3. Stabilisation and the Special Case of Mathematics

Over time, repeated successful reconstructions lead to **stabilisation**: certain compressions and trajectories become entrenched as shared symbolic systems — language, notation, conceptual frameworks. Mathematics is one such stabilised domain, in which compression, generation, and transformation are tightly constrained. Anomalies in mathematics and physics (infinities, singularities, renormalisation) are reinterpreted as **boundary phenomena**: arising when symbolic constructions extend beyond the domain in which stable measurement-based compressions can be formed.

## Reflection Questions

1. **The Generon and provenance:** If numbers are generated through finite procedures rather than assumed to exist, what does this imply for the provenance of a mathematical claim? How does the Generon relate to the tilde (~) notation used throughout FSM documents?

2. **Meaning and trajectory:** Identify an instance in your own experience where the same symbol (word, equation, diagram) produced different meanings in different contexts. How does the trajectory account explain this, compared to a fixed-correspondence account?

3. **The Geofinite Continuum:** The Geofinite Continuum is described as a structural inference — not directly accessible, inferred from the limits of measurement. What other concepts in science or philosophy serve a similar structural role? What distinguishes the Geofinite Continuum from classical notions of the infinite or the real?

4. **Stabilisation and convention:** If mathematics is a stabilised symbolic system rather than a timeless structure, what are the implications for mathematical truth? Does a theorem proven within a stabilised system have a different status than one which extends to the boundary?

5. **The child's gesture:** The essay ends with a child pointing at a tree — an act that instantiates the entire six-step process. Choose a different everyday act of communication and trace it through the same six steps: Continuum encounter → Generon → Compression → Projection → Reconstruction → Stabilisation.
