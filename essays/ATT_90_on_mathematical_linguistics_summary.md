## Core Claim

A mathematical name — *Abelian*, *Euclidean*, *Gaussian*, *Hamiltonian* — is not itself mathematical content. It is a compressed pointer into a previously stabilised linguistic trajectory. The essay distinguishes three things that mathematical speech typically presents as one: the historical name, the operational relation, and the apparent object that seems to bear the name as an intrinsic property. What is ordinarily called mathematical abstraction is better understood as symbolic compression: many operational instances become a recognised relation, the relation receives a name, and the name subsequently enters new symbolic constructions as though it named an independent entity. The name is not the mathematics. It is an admissible label whose trajectory remains recoverable. The next step is not to discard the name but to unfold it.

---

## Overview

Mathematics presents itself as a language of unusual precision, yet its vocabulary is filled with inherited names: Abelian, Euclidean, Cartesian, Gaussian, Hermitian, Noetherian, Hamiltonian, Lagrangian, Bayesian, Markovian, and many others. To the initiated mathematician these terms are efficient; they appear to identify familiar structures almost immediately. To someone outside the established linguistic community they reveal almost nothing of the operations, relations, or procedures they supposedly describe.

The essay begins from this simple observation: a term such as *Abelian* is not itself mathematical content. It is a historical name functioning as a highly compressed pointer into a previously learned network of meanings. From this starting point, mathematical naming is examined as a linguistic process in which procedures become relations, relations acquire names, and names eventually come to appear as properties of mathematical objects. Arthur F. Bentley's *Linguistic Analysis of Mathematics* (1932) provides an intriguing historical point of contact. The essay does not attempt a full analysis of Bentley's work but establishes a trajectory from which such an analysis can later be connected more formally with Finite Symbolic Mechanics, Functional Symbolic Trajectories, and Finite Symbolic Logic.

The essay is explicitly described as a preliminary linguistic investigation rather than a completed formal argument — a measurement rather than a theorem.

## Structure and Key Argument

**The Name.** The word *Abelian* is used to open the analysis. Within mathematics the term is completely ordinary: an Abelian group is a group whose operation is commutative (a ○ b = b ○ a). To a trained mathematician the word is efficient — a considerable amount of information appears to arrive with eight letters. But the letters themselves contain almost none of that information. There is nothing about the word *Abelian* that tells a reader that two operations may be performed in either order while preserving the specified relation. It points to Abel — more precisely, to a historical and linguistic community within which the name *Abel* has acquired a particular mathematical trajectory. For the uninitiated reader, the same word activates almost nothing. The marks are identical. The difference lies in the trajectory available to the reader.

**The Mathematical Club.** Specialised communities inevitably develop specialised languages. Once a community has learned the required language, enormously complicated relations can be communicated economically. The price of this economy is less often noticed: terms begin to function as code words. The expert sees "Abelian group" and reconstructs an extensive body of assumptions, definitions, and allowable operations. The novice sees an unfamiliar adjective attached to an unfamiliar noun. The difference does not lie in the marks on the page; it lies in the trajectory available to the reader.

**From Person to Property.** Eponymous mathematics makes the compression trajectory particularly easy to see: Abel → Abelian (a person's name becomes an adjective) → Abelian group (the adjective attaches to a mathematical noun) → *G* is Abelian (the adjective begins to behave as the name of an intrinsic property). The same broad movement can be seen repeatedly: Euclid → Euclidean, Gauss → Gaussian, Hilbert → Hilbert space, Hamilton → Hamiltonian, Lagrange → Lagrangian, Noether → Noetherian. The trajectory by which the term acquired its meaning gradually disappears, leaving behind what appears to be a stable mathematical object or property. Schematically: procedure → repeatable relation → name → property → apparent object. The linguistic history becomes compressed. Eventually the compression becomes so successful that it is difficult to see that compression has taken place at all.

**Unpacking the Compression.** The word *Abelian* can be treated as a compressed representation. Unpacking it produces something much less mysterious: a collection of admissible symbols or elements, a specified operation, performance of the operation on admissible pairs, reversal of the order of the operands, and comparison of the registered results. Schematically: G → {a, b, ...} → ○ → a ○ b → b ○ a → comparison. The mathematical content lies in this network of operations and relations. *Abelian* merely provides a convenient address. This suggests an important distinction: a name can be operationally useful without being foundationally explanatory. An eponym may be an admissible label, but it is not an admissible explanation.

**What Remains When the Name Is Removed?** A useful test: remove the name. What remains? If we remove *Abelian*, the operational relation remains available. The name is dispensable. The operational trajectory is not. Mathematical education usually proceeds in the opposite direction: a term is introduced, given a definition, and subsequently used as though the term carries the definition within itself. Initially Abelian → definition. After repeated use: definition → Abelian. The compressed label becomes sufficient for an expert reader. This is extraordinarily powerful — one of the reasons mathematics can construct long symbolic arguments without repeatedly expanding every earlier definition. But the same efficiency can conceal the structure through which meaning is being carried.

**Mathematics and Its Surrounding Language.** Arthur F. Bentley's *Linguistic Analysis of Mathematics* (1932) explicitly turned attention towards the linguistic conditions surrounding mathematical symbolism — not merely with isolated notation, but with the language in which symbols are introduced, interpreted, related, and communicated. At first sight it is tempting to divide mathematical discourse into two regions: ordinary language | mathematical notation. But the boundary is much less obvious than it appears. Mathematical symbols themselves must be learned. Their permissible operations must be taught. Their meanings change historically. Their uses depend upon convention. Their interpretation depends upon surrounding symbols. The sign = cannot function merely by being present on a page: a reader must possess a trajectory of use that determines what sort of relation is being asserted. The same is true of +, 0, 1, ∈, or any other mathematical mark. The apparent distinction between linguistic and mathematical symbols may conceal a deeper continuity: both are symbols, both obtain their practical meaning through use, both depend upon established relations, both participate in historically developed systems of interpretation.

**Is Mathematics Embedded in Language?** The conventional picture: mathematical structure → symbolic notation → linguistic explanation. Under this view, the mathematical structure exists independently, while language is merely employed to communicate it. But another possibility is available: mathematics may itself be a specialised form of symbolic language, giving something closer to symbolic distinction → repeatable operation → stable convention → mathematics. Under this interpretation, mathematics does not escape the problems of language merely by adopting specialist notation. Its language is more tightly constrained, but it remains language.

**The Appearance of Mathematical Things.** The problem extends beyond eponyms. Words such as *point*, *line*, *set*, *space*, *field*, *function*, *dimension* no longer appear as eponyms. Their histories are buried much more deeply. A point seems to be a thing. A set seems to be a thing. A function seems to be a thing. Yet each is introduced through linguistic and symbolic operations. A definition is established, permitted manipulations are specified, examples are given, relations are repeated, a community stabilises usage. Eventually the term begins to appear self-evident. The word ceases to look like a linguistic construction and starts to look like the name of something that was waiting to be discovered.

**From Operation to Noun.** The problem connects with a much wider tendency within language: processes are repeatedly converted into nouns, relations become things, activities become entities, regularities become properties. Mathematics intensifies this tendency because it requires stable handles that can be reused within extended symbolic constructions. Schematically: many operational instances → recognised relation → name → classification → new relations. This is an extraordinarily effective form of compression. But it may also encourage attribution of an existence to the compressed symbol that originally belonged only to the operations through which the symbol was produced.

**Compression Rather Than Abstraction.** What is ordinarily called mathematical abstraction may instead be understood more cautiously as symbolic compression. When several different operational systems exhibit a sufficiently similar registered relation, rather than continually restating the similarity, a symbolic label is assigned: A₁, ..., Aₙ → L. The new symbol L allows the previously identified relation to be carried economically into subsequent reasoning. Nothing mysterious need have been extracted from the systems. Nothing need have entered an abstract realm. A finite symbolic community has recognised a repeatable relation and compressed it into another symbol. The new symbol then acquires its own trajectory. Schematically: registered relations → classification → symbolic compression → new symbol. What is ordinarily called abstraction may therefore contain a substantial and sometimes overlooked linguistic component.

**The Reader Reconstructs the Mathematics.** A mathematical expression does not deliver an identical meaning to every observer. The reader reconstructs its meaning using previously learned symbolic relations. The physical inscription has not changed; what differs is the available history of interaction with that inscription and the surrounding symbolic system. Meaning therefore appears less like a substance contained inside a symbol and more like a trajectory capable of being reactivated. The experienced mathematician does not inspect each symbol anew: a previously established path is followed. This may explain something of the peculiar density of advanced mathematics — a short expression can activate an enormous network of prior relations, but only for somebody whose previous symbolic history permits the reconstruction. The page is therefore not carrying all of the mathematics. Part of the mathematics is being supplied by the reader.

**Towards Finite Symbolic Mechanics.** What is actually present when mathematics is performed, written, communicated, or measured? There are finite marks, distinctions between marks, rules governing how marks may be combined, histories through which those rules have been established, communities that recognise particular operations as admissible, and readers who reconstruct trajectories from finite inscriptions. From an FSM perspective, the name itself cannot provide foundational admissibility. The relevant question becomes: what finite symbolic trajectory allows the named relation to be produced, distinguished, registered, and subsequently recognised? *Abelian*, considered alone, cannot answer this. It is instead a compressed pointer towards a trajectory already stabilised elsewhere.

**A Possible Reversal.** Traditional mathematical discourse often proceeds by naming and then manipulating named objects. FSM suggests that it may sometimes be valuable to run the trajectory backwards. Instead of name → property → mathematical object, one might ask: symbolic object → permitted operations → registered relations → name. The second form preserves the provenance of the name. The noun remains attached to the process that produced it. The label remains visibly a label.

**Functional Symbolic Trajectories.** The term *Abelian* can be considered not as a container holding a mathematical meaning but as a node capable of reactivating a learned trajectory: "Abelian" → prior language → definitions → operations → relations → ... The apparent stability of the word arises from the repeatability of this reconstruction within a sufficiently trained community.

**Towards a Linguistic Analysis of Mathematics.** Bentley's work may provide an important historical bridge. His *Linguistic Analysis of Mathematics* (1932) was produced long before contemporary neural networks, information theory, modern digital computation, or the present framework of Finite Symbolic Mechanics. It therefore approached the linguistic problem from a very different historical position. Reading Bentley may reveal earlier attempts to identify the dependence of mathematical practice upon language without importing the conceptual vocabulary developed here.

**The Measurement This Essay Makes.** The essay has attempted a measurement rather than a theorem. The quantity under investigation was the interval between a name and the finite procedure that the name is later taken to contain. What the comparison distinguishes is not a person from a theorem but a label from a measurement. The equality a ○ b = b ○ a is already a recorded distinction. The word *Abelian* compresses that recorded distinction so that it can be carried forward without repeating the procedure. The compression is admissible. It becomes misleading only when the record is treated as an intrinsic property that no longer requires conditions of production. Three things that ordinary mathematical speech presents as one have been separated: the historical name, the operational relation, and the apparent object that later seems to bear the name as a property. The result is modest. The name is recoverable as provenance. The relation is recoverable as a repeatable comparison. The object-appearance is recoverable as the success of compression. The next step is to unfold the name.
