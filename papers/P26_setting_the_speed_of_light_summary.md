## Abstract

The sentence "the speed of light is exactly 299 792 458 m s⁻¹" is routinely presented as though it reports an infinitely precise observation of nature. It does not. The metre and the second existed before this numerical value could be measured. In 1975, 299 792 458 m s⁻¹ was recommended as a measured value whose uncertainty was limited principally by the realisation of the metre. In 1983, the same integer was set as exact by resolution of the General Conference on Weights and Measures, and the metre was redefined through it. Measurement produced the value; the Committee set the certainty. Thereafter, no experiment expressed within the present International System of Units could revise the numerical value of *c*. Any uncertainty or discrepancy had to be assigned to the realisation of the units, the apparatus, the measurement pathway or the surrounding theory. This paper reconstructs that reversal and argues that certainty was not discovered in nature but placed in the symbols, after which material uncertainty was made answerable to the exact symbolic reference.

## Core Claim

The exactness of c = 299 792 458 m s⁻¹ is not a fact about light. It is a consequence of setting. Before 1983, the numerical value was downstream of measurement and could in principle be revised by a better observation. After 1983, the same integer became upstream — a defining relation that governs how every SI length measurement must be expressed. Uncertainty did not disappear; it was expelled from the defining number and redistributed across the measured world.

The paper's central distinction: *Measurement produced the value. The Committee set the certainty.*

## Structure and Key Argument

The paper proceeds through seven movements, tracing the historical trajectory that produced and then hardened the integer 299 792 458.

**Before certainty.** Prior to 1983, the ordinary direction of empirical correction held:

> interaction → measurement → number with uncertainty → possible revision

The 1889 platinum-iridium metre bar had extent. Temperature, engraved lines, material stability, optical comparison, and the condition of the artefact all entered the measurement. Every transfer from the prototype carried a chain of comparison and uncertainty.

**The metre is transferred into a count.** In 1960, one metre was set equal to 1 650 763.73 wavelengths of krypton-86 radiation. The metre came first; the count was fitted to it to preserve the existing standard as closely as possible. The 1967 second was similarly set as 9 192 631 770 periods of caesium-133 radiation. By the early 1970s, c = λf could be formed from optical frequency (carrying the second) and wavelength (carrying the metre).

**The value before it was set.** In 1975, the 15th CGPM recommended c = 299 792 458 m s⁻¹ with an estimated relative uncertainty of ±4×10⁻⁹. The direction of reference remained explicit:

> realised metre + realised second → measured value of c

The measurement could still answer back. The integer was a recommended centre of a finite measurement interval, not an exact number.

**1983: the Committee sets the certainty.** The 17th CGPM adopted a new metre definition: the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during 1/299 792 458 of a second. The Committee's own resolution records the decisive reasoning: measurements of c had become limited principally by the realisation of the existing metre; setting the integer as exact would produce no appreciable discontinuity in the unit of length. The Committee did not perform an experiment that eliminated the remaining uncertainty. It set the number so that uncertainty could no longer attach to it.

**Why "set" matters.** Official metrological language usually says the value was *fixed*. The paper insists on *set*: it retains the actor, the action, and the resulting state. A committee sets a value. A clock is set against a reference. Something set in stone is removed from ordinary revision. The number 299 792 458 became perfect not because measurement reached perfection, but because the symbolic system ceased to permit a remainder.

**Where the uncertainty went.** The new hierarchy:

> c ≡ 299 792 458 m s⁻¹ exactly  
> ↓  
> all material realisations, instruments and observations carry uncertainty

Before 1983, uncertainty stood *above* the numerical value and could require its correction. After 1983, certainty was placed *in* the numerical relation, and uncertainty was made subordinate to it. An experiment returning 299 792 457.8 m s⁻¹ cannot revise the speed of light within the present SI; the discrepancy must be placed in the realised second, the apparatus, the uncertainty analysis, or the theoretical framework.

**A God-like number inside the system.** The defining integer is placed beyond the reach of the measurements it governs. If a conflict appears, the surrounding account must change while the central relation remains untouched. This is not a claim about metrologists worshipping numbers; it is a claim about the geometry of authority. A convention of representation, once set, becomes an ontological declaration if its provenance is forgotten.

**Closure and the direction of later physics.** When foundational symbols are exact and protected, an observation that does not fit cannot be allowed to revise them. The trajectory takes the form:

> set symbolic foundations → observation does not fit → additional entity, correction or framework → foundations remain set

Dark matter offers a structural illustration: observed galactic motions do not agree with the established description when only registered matter is included, so an additional entity is inferred to preserve the larger framework. The setting of c does not cause dark matter, but it exemplifies the geometry by which a practical decision can harden into the apparent furniture of the universe.

**The responsible statement.** The paper proposes the full provenance-bearing claim that the familiar sentence suppresses:

> *Within the International System of Units, the numerical value of the speed of light in vacuum was set by resolution of the General Conference on Weights and Measures at exactly 299 792 458 m s⁻¹. The value was selected to preserve continuity with the previously realised metre and cannot be revised by further SI measurement while the present definitions remain in force.*

This sentence is longer because the history is part of the meaning. Removing it produces a more efficient statement but also a different claim.
